From spectrum to structure: Identifying molecules with Mass Spec Skills for Google Antigravity
Built in collaboration with analytical chemists and using community-developed tools, Mass Spec Skills empowers scientists using Google Antigravity to interpret tandem mass spectra, lay out chemical evidence, and match frontier-level retrieval accuracy even with a lightweight model.
Every sample of blood, soil or plant tissue contains a vast array of small molecules, such as nutrients, hormones, drugs and their breakdown products, pollutants, or bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential. A modern mass spectrometer can detect tens of thousands of these molecular signals in a single drop of sample extract, measuring the masses of charged molecules and, in tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), the fragments they break into.
These measurements, however, do not directly reveal the molecular structure. Researchers cannot immediately tell which compound produced a signal and relate it to its biological role or downstream use cases.
To fully map the diversity of small molecules, which remains largely uncharted, researchers need ways to translate mass spectra to molecular structures at scale. The standard approach is to match each spectrum against reference spectra from previously measured molecules. Yet even after a decade of expanding reference libraries, spectral searches annotate on average only about 5% of measured spectra, and 10% for human samples; the rest has been called the "dark matter" of mass spectrometry.
Many of the molecules producing these unannotated signals are already listed in public chemical databases, but their spectra have not been recorded, so there is no reference spectrum to compare against. Elucidating them instead requires inferring the structure directly from the observed fragment ions.
Analytical chemists have a well-tested, step-by-step workflow for narrowing down candidate structures from tandem (MS/MS) spectra: check spectral reference libraries, find the molecular formula and retrieve candidate structures from chemical databases, and then test every candidate against observed fragment peaks.
The tools for each step exist and are openly available, but running the full workflow would take a domain expert a significant amount of time for every single spectrum. Because a single study can produce thousands of spectra, manual analysis cannot keep up, leaving most of these signals unannotated.
Today, we are sharing Mass Spec Skills for Google Antigravity, a suite of domain skills that pair Gemini's chemical reasoning with the ability to run established mass spectrometry tools and query public chemical databases on the user's behalf to fulfil their request. Antigravity runs these skills, combines their results, and reasons about the chemistry, laying out the evidence for a chemist to review.
For molecules that are already catalogued in a database, identification is much faster. For undiscovered molecules, the agent still helps: it narrows down the formula and the parts of the structure supported by the fragments to guide full structure elucidation. A language model cannot do this on its own, since matching fragments to a structure takes precise mass calculations, searches across databases of millions of compounds, and simulations of how bonds break. Pairing Gemini’s reasoning with dedicated chemistry tools brings those steps into one workflow.
Researchers can describe their task and any instrument-specific tolerances in plain language without having to configure each underlying tool. The agent selects and chains the relevant skills, lowering the technical barrier to entry for scientists less familiar with computational tools. From a single prompt, the agent can take an unannotated tandem mass spectrum all the way to a proposed chemical structure and an auditable report (Figure 2).

From raw spectrum to structure end-to-end
To see how the chemical evidence fits together in practice, consider the negative-mode tandem mass spectrum from the end-to-end run in Figure 2 (precursor [M−H]− at m/z 395.1387; Figure 3).
Negative-ion spectra are traditionally much harder to annotate than positive-ion recordings due to limited reference libraries and less well-characterised fragmentation mechanisms. With spectral library lookup disabled, the agent used SIRIUS to pin down the molecular formula C20H21ClN6O. In the fragmentation tree, the molecule splits cleanly into two complementary halves at m/z 162.1037 ([C9H12N3]−) and m/z 232.0283 ([C11H7ClN3O]−), whose formulas add up to the intact precursor. From the m/z 232.0283 half, sequential neutral losses of HCl and isocyanic acid (HNCO) in both orders form a closed loop indicating a central urea linkage.
With the molecular formula and these fragmentation clues established, the agent retrieved and scored all 845 C20H21ClN6O isomers in PubChem. Several high-scoring candidates, including a fused triazolobenzazepinol isomer (CID 154638626), accounted for 97.7% of the summed fragment intensity across 14 of the 19 preprocessed peaks, so the Gemini-powered agent tested whether they were chemically consistent with the observed losses. The triazolobenzazepinol isomer lacks a urea linkage, leaving it with no route to lose HNCO, and despite carrying a secondary alcohol, the spectrum has only baseline noise at the expected water-loss masses (m/z 377.1281 and m/z 214.0178). Filtering the 845 isomers for structures with a central urea bond that cleaves into the two measured halves ruled out close amide isomers such as the runner-up (CID 55558151; Figure 4) and narrowed the list to five urea regioisomers, where secondary fragments from the chlorophenyl-pyrazole side (m/z 206.0485 and m/z 126.0114) singled out the true molecule (CID 137835447). The verified structure and both rejected non-urea isomers accounted for the same 97.7% of summed fragment intensity, so distinguishing them required testing their fragmentation pathways.
Spectral coverage measures how much peak intensity a candidate matches by mass alone, without testing whether its functional groups and bond connectivity can plausibly form those fragments. Checking candidates against diagnostic neutral losses and bond cleavages, just as an analytical chemist would, lets Antigravity rule out high-scoring mismatches on its own and verify the final structure.
Reliable library identification and verification, even with a lightweight model
Even when a compound's reference spectrum is already stored in a public library, finding and verifying it is harder than simply giving an AI agent internet access. To test how reliably Antigravity handles end-to-end identification and verification, we evaluated it on 100 compounds sampled from the Enamine REAL MS/MS collection in MoNA (with structures also catalogued in PubChem), after filtering out under-fragmented recordings such as intact precursors or dimers that barely broke apart.
We compared three set-ups: (1) Antigravity with no internet access or skills (to test what the models can deduce from the peak list alone), (2) Antigravity with user-permitted internet access to query public libraries and databases on the user's behalf, and (3) Antigravity with that same access plus the Mass Spec Skills, across three models: the lightweight Gemini 3.8 Flash and two frontier models, Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 4 Carbon.
With no internet access or skills, accuracy remained below 4% across all three models, confirming that they cannot reliably deduce molecular structures from the peak list alone. Giving the agent limited internet access allowed it to query online libraries, but custom search scripts written from scratch proved brittle around precursor windows, adducts, and noisy peaks, causing the frontier models to miss a quarter to a third of the spectra and the lightweight model to miss more than three-quarters.
With the Mass Spec Skills added, all three models identified and verified the exact molecule in around 97% of attempts, with the lightweight model matching the frontier models' accuracy (Figure 5a).
Skills also cut token cost and effort. Without internet or skills, the models used the most tokens per attempt trying to deduce structures from raw peak lists, and with internet access alone they still spent substantial tokens improvising API queries and debugging one-off scripts. With the Mass Spec Skills, the agent in Antigravity calls validated, ready-made skills instead, cutting generated tokens per attempt across every model: by nearly four times on Flash, by half on Argon, and by nearly a fifth on Carbon (Figure 5b).
A collaborative analytical assistant
Antigravity’s Mass Spec Skills pair mass spectrometry tools trusted by the community with transparent agentic reasoning, performing the time-consuming groundwork of spectral interpretation and keeping the chemist in control of decisions requiring scientific judgement.
However, there are some questions that cannot be answered from an LC-MS/MS spectrum at all, such as mirror-image molecules and some closely related isomers. These molecules all break apart in nearly identical ways; telling them apart requires other measurements, such as chromatographic retention time, ion mobility, or NMR.
Genuinely new molecules, including many natural products, are not in any existing database, so Antigravity cannot retrieve or confirm a structure on its own. It does, however, give an informed starting point for chemists, with a predicted formula, peak assignments, and a detailed annotation table.
Scaling automated spectral interpretation from single spectra to full metabolomics studies can help researchers identify far more of the unknown small molecules across human health, environmental science, and natural-product discovery.
