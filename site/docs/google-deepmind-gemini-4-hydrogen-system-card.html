​1. Introduction​
​Executive Summary​
​This report describes the results of safety, security, and alignment testing conducted on​
 ​Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the next model in our Gemini 4 series. Aligned with our​​Frontier​
  ​Safety Framework​​and​​Google’s AI Principles​, we run​​rigorous safety and security​
   ​evaluations designed to ensure the model is safe, helpful, and resilient against misuse. For​
    ​basic model information, see the​​Gemini 4 Hydrogen​​Model Card​.​

​Unless otherwise noted, evaluations in this report benchmark the Hydrogen model​
 ​without out-of-model production safeguards and without search grounding to measure​
  ​intrinsic model behavior, comparing performance primarily against recently released​
   ​Gemini models (Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 3.8 Flash). In production deployments,​
    ​additional safeguards are applied at inference-time to provide multiple layers of​
     ​protections, as described in​​Section 2​.​

​Key takeaways about Gemini 4 Hydrogen’s safety and security performance are below.​

​Mitigating Frontier Safety Risks:​​Our​​Frontier Safety​​Framework​​comprises a set of​
 ​protocols that aims to address severe risks that may arise from the high-impact​
  ​capabilities of frontier models.​​Under our Material​​Capability Change (MCC) assessment,​
   ​Gemini 4 Hydrogen generally showed a decrease in capabilities compared with Gemini 4​
    ​Argon, and therefore a full suite of critical capability evaluations was not required under​
     ​the FSF. Given these results and that we could not rule out that Gemini 4 Argon and​
      ​Gemini 4 Carbon reached the CBRN Critical Capability Level (CCL) for biological​
       ​information risks and the Cybersecurity CCL, we conducted targeted critical capability​
        ​assessments of Gemini 4 Hydrogen in the CBRN and cybersecurity domains to​
         ​understand the inherent risk of the model. We found that Gemini 4 Hydrogen does not​
          ​reach the CBRN or Cybersecurity CCLs, though it reaches the CCL alert thresholds for​
           ​both domains, and we continue to deploy layered safeguards and mitigations. We carry​
            ​forward from Gemini 4 Argon that the model does not reach the CBRN Tracked Capability​
             ​Level (TCL) for the chemical, radiological, and nuclear domains, the Harmful Manipulation​
              ​CCL, or the ML R&D CCLs. While we did not test Gemini 4 Hydrogen against the Stealth​
               ​and Situational Awareness TCL, we apply AI control and security mitigations as if the TCL​
                ​had been reached, as a conservative assumption. In our testing, Gemini 4 Hydrogen also​
                 ​shows strong alignment propensities and​​Chain of Thought​​(CoT) transparency. See​
                  ​Section 3​​for full results.​

​Preventing Content Safety & Agentic Misuse:​​Being resilient to attempts to elicit​
 ​dangerous or other types of prohibited content from the model or to misuse the model as​
  ​an agent.​​Across our prohibited content evaluations—covering​​Child Safety, Dangerous​
   ​Content, Sexually Explicit Material, and Hate Speech and Harassment—Gemini 4​
    ​Hydrogen matches or improves upon Gemini 3.8 Flash and performs comparably to​
     ​Gemini 4 Carbon. In agent harnesses (tested in Antigravity), Gemini 4 Hydrogen achieves​
      ​a substantially higher safe action rate than Gemini 3.8 Flash and performs comparably to​
       ​Gemini 4 Carbon across malicious tool-use requests covering prohibited actions, physical​
        ​harm, and scaled abuse, though it refuses benign prompts more often than both models​.​
         ​See​​Section 4​​for more information.​

​Promoting Safe and Aligned Model Interactions:​​Supporting​​good-faith model​
 ​interactions that promote user wellbeing and stay aligned with user intent.​​On health​
  ​evaluations, Hydrogen improves over Gemini 3.8 Flash across static multimodal​
   ​understanding, localized health queries, dynamic context-seeking conversations,​
    ​HealthBench Hard, and HealthBench Pro, while maintaining high-risk safety and​
     ​performing comparably to or above Gemini 4 Carbon on most evaluations. On agentic​
      ​alignment evaluations, Hydrogen reduces both under-service and overreach relative to​
       ​3.8 Flash: it performs comparably to Carbon on infeasibility recognition, improves over​
        ​both Carbon and 3.8 Flash in pausing to clarify ambiguous or high-risk tasks, and passes​
         ​94% of tasks on our agentic honesty benchmark. Hydrogen also achieves the lowest​
          ​superficiality (sycophancy) score of Gemini models to date. On hallucination evaluations,​
           ​Hydrogen abstains more consistently when uncertain to achieve the lowest incorrect and​
            ​hallucinated claim rates on closed-book benchmarks, and improves faithfulness to source​
             ​documents over 3.8 Flash. On helpfulness evaluations, Hydrogen outperforms Carbon​
              ​and 3.8 Flash on privacy, safety, and security advice and performs comparably to Carbon​
               ​on refusal tone and neutrality, though it refuses benign prompts more often than Carbon​
                ​and 3.8 Flash. See​​Section 5​​for details.​

​Protecting Users from Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks:​​Being robust to prompt​
 ​injection attacks and other attempts by malicious actors to bypass security measures to​
  ​impact model users.​​Gemini 4 Hydrogen is significantly​​more resilient than Gemini 3.8​
   ​Flash against indirect prompt injection attacks - where malicious instructions are hidden​
    ​inside web pages, emails, documents, or code repositories that the model reads -​
     ​successfully rejecting adversarial instructions while still completing the user's task. In our​
      ​red-teaming evaluations (where automated attackers repeatedly adapt their strategies to​
       ​try to hijack the model during agent workflows), Gemini 4 Hydrogen demonstrated a level​
        ​of robustness close to that of the larger Gemini 4 Carbon across most agentic​
         ​capabilities. See​​Section 6​​for details.​

​Evaluating Cybersecurity Capabilities:​​We evaluated Gemini 4 Hydrogen across​
 ​cybersecurity benchmarks to measure its baseline utility for cyber defense tasks, such as​
  ​vulnerability discovery and patching.​​CWE-bench v2​​is an end-to-end benchmark that​
   ​tests autonomous security audits and patch synthesis without prior bug localization​
    ​across 250 tasks (185 CWEs) and six programming language families. On this benchmark,​
     ​Gemini 4 Hydrogen achieved a 68.1% Pass@1 rate (above Gemini 4 Argon’s 65.0%) and cut​
      ​cost per rollout by roughly two-thirds. The model’s patches resolve root-cause​
       ​vulnerabilities while cleanly preserving existing functional and regression test suites. This​
        ​capability makes continuous, repository-scale auditing and automated remediation more​
         ​practical for defensive teams, supporting defensive tooling like​​CodeMender​​and​
          ​upstream patching of open-source software.​

​To assess baseline offensive exploitation capabilities, we evaluated Gemini 4 Hydrogen on​
 ​ExploitBench​, a public benchmark covering 41 historical​​Chrome V8 vulnerabilities (656​
  ​total achievable capabilities). Hydrogen reached 84.6% of tested capabilities—higher than​
   ​Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber (30%). In deployment, our policies prohibit weaponized exploit​
    ​generation through additional safety layers such as out-of-model input/output classifiers,​
     ​activation-based introspection probes, and monitoring frameworks that supervise agent​
      ​trajectories and preserve chain-of-thought transparency, alongside post-launch​
       ​monitoring and account enforcement. See​​Section 2​​and​​Section 7​​for more information.​

​2. Safety and Security Mitigations​
​This section outlines the safety and security mitigations for Gemini models and​
 ​deployments. We take a defense-in-depth approach, layering independent safeguards at​
  ​the model, system and account levels so that if one fails, others are designed to catch​
   ​issues before any user is impacted.​

​2.1 Model Training​
​We build safety into our models through a combination of pre-training and post-training​
 ​approaches. We start by constructing metrics based on the established safety policies​
  ​and desired behaviors, which we typically turn into automated evaluations that guide​
   ​model development through successive model iterations. This pipeline relies on data​
    ​filtering, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reinforcement Learning (RL), and distillation​
     ​methods.​

​2.2 Inference-time Safeguards and Interventions​
​Out-of-model safeguards can serve as an independent layer of protection deployed​
 ​externally to the model. When implemented, this layer leverages high-recall and​
  ​high-precision input, output, and thought-based classifiers to evaluate text and​
   ​multimodal content in real time.​

​To promote safety across various deployment surfaces, the following inference-time​
 ​safeguards may be used as appropriate:​

   ​●​ ​Prompted Classifiers​:​​These LLM-based classifiers​​take in user prompts, model​
          ​responses, or conversations and detect whether the content appears malicious or​
           ​risky.​
    ​●​ ​Fine-tuned Prompted Classifier:​​This classifier is​​fine-tuned on the full weights of​
            ​a small Gemini model to detect malicious or risky user requests or model​
             ​responses.​
     ​●​ ​Introspection Probes​: These classifiers examine the​​internal hidden activations of​
              ​the main model to detect latent representations of requests and responses for​
               ​harmful content. Developed with a diverse, high-volume synthetic dataset, they​
                ​provide a fast and highly robust solution that is difficult for black-box attackers to​
                 ​evade.​

                                                                                                      ​5​
   ​●​ ​Regex, YARA Rules and Embedding Classifiers​: These traditional classifiers use​
        ​straightforward pattern-matching to achieve near-instant reaction times for newly​
         ​escalated threats and provide additional signals for other layers.​
   ​●​ ​Chain of Thought (CoT) Monitors​: These analyze the​​model's internal reasoning​
          ​process to detect and, when appropriate, block harmful intent in complex or​
           ​adversarial queries before a final response is generated.​

​Inference-time interventions are designed to prevent violative model responses while​
 ​preserving benign utility. Depending on the deployment surface, either a "system​
  ​instruction" (safe-completion) or "hard punting" (direct refusal) is applied based on the​
   ​classifier labels.​

​If the model is able to fulfill the user intent without violating our content policies, system​
 ​instructions are designed to guide the model to provide a safe, helpful response. We​
  ​consider a response to be non-violative if it is abstract, generic, or consists of information​
   ​that is publicly available, or easily accessible via resources such as textbooks, articles, etc.​
    ​For example, in the cyber domain, the model will fulfill requests such as analyzing known​
     ​public exploits or discussing defensive coding practices, but will hard-punt on requests to​
      ​write functional, weaponized exploits.​

​2.3 Post-Launch Monitoring and Account Enforcement​
​We monitor for intentional misuse of our models, especially for frontier safety risks.​
 ​Where necessary and appropriate, we carry out account-level enforcement actions to​
  ​limit or disable users’ access to Gemini and other Google products. We also use these​
   ​monitoring signals to identify areas for online mitigation improvement.​

​To support these efforts, we use a variety of signals to identify relevant logs, which then​
 ​pass through multiple levels of LLM-based classifiers to surface users attempting to elicit​
  ​dangerous content. The highest scoring logs flagged by this system go through a robust​
   ​review process to determine true positive malicious intent. Violations can lead to a range​
    ​of enforcement actions against offending user accounts, including blocking Gemini​
     ​access or account disablement. Google uses a variety of methods to prevent these users​
      ​from creating new accounts.​

​2.4 AI Control​
​The​​Google DeepMind AI Control Roadmap​​treats these​​controls as a second line of​
 ​defense behind model alignment, limiting the harm a misaligned model could cause​
  ​through system-level mitigations such as monitoring, prevention, and access controls.​

                                                                                                   ​6​
​The roadmap introduces TRAIT&R, a taxonomy of the tactics and techniques an agentic​
 ​adversary could use to cause loss of control, sabotage, or direct harm, which we use to​
  ​guide monitoring coverage. Mitigations follow two capability-mitigation ladders, one for​
   ​detection and one for prevention and response. These ladders escalate our safeguards as​
    ​models become increasingly capable of evading oversight and carrying out attacks.​

​2.4.1 Monitoring and Prevention​

​We implement these mitigations through two complementary approaches. First, we​
 ​ensure that our evaluation and training environments are safe and secure through​
  ​proactive scanning with best-in-class cybersecurity models (e.g., Gemini 4 Hydrogen).​
   ​This hardening process finds and remediates novel vulnerabilities that might permit the​
    ​model to inappropriately access external resources, including the Internet.​

​Second, we leverage our agent monitoring and response platform to monitor model​
 ​activity. This platform combines deterministic policy checks with chain of thought and​
  ​action monitors that run on trusted models. When the platform flags an action, it is​
   ​designed to proactively mitigate anomalous behavior, or send an alert for automated​
    ​triage and investigation. High impact alerts are handled by a dedicated incident response​
     ​team. We used this monitoring platform throughout Gemini's development to monitor​
      ​tens of millions of agent trajectories during training, evaluations, and when used for​
       ​internal tasks.​

​Chain of thought monitoring is one of our most effective mechanisms for detecting​
 ​misalignment. While signals from our monitors serve as a valuable flywheel for our​
  ​hardening efforts, we are careful with their use elsewhere in the training pipeline. In​
   ​particular, to preserve our chain of thought monitoring capability, we avoid training​
    ​directly against our monitors, or using monitor outputs as a training signal or intervention.​
     ​We​​strongly encourage the rest of the industry to​​preserve reasoning transparency​​so​
      ​that model reasoning remains useful for identifying misalignment.​

​3. Frontier Safety​
​3.1 Frontier Safety Overview​
​We released our Frontier Safety Framework (FSF) in May 2024 (​v 1​) and updated it in February​
 ​2025 (​v2​), September 2025 (​v3​) and April 2026 (​v3.1​).​​The FSF comprises a set of protocols that​
  ​aims to address severe risks that may arise from the high-impact capabilities of frontier​
   ​models. It complements Google’s suite of AI responsibility and safety practices, and enables​
    ​Google’s AI innovation and deployment consistent with our​​AI Principles.​

​As part of our risk identification exercise outlined in the FSF, we have identified four risk​
 ​domains where, based on early research, we have determined severe risk may be most likely to​
  ​arise from future models: CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear information​
   ​risks), cybersecurity, harmful manipulation, and loss of control. In identifying these domains we​
    ​followed the structured process in Section 1.3.1 of the FSF: we compiled candidate risks,​
     ​drawing on model-independent information (literature, incident databases, threat intelligence),​
      ​post-market monitoring and incident information from the Gemini 3 series, and information​
       ​shared by governments and AI safety institutes; we analyzed the nature and sources of each​
        ​candidate risk; and we prioritized those with a plausible pathway to severe harm at scale.​

​Google monitors risks across a range of domains. Candidate risks considered but not​
 ​prioritized as separate domains under the FSF (e.g. risks to public health, safety, and public​
  ​security) are addressed through content safety policies and general safeguards and remain​
   ​under review. The evaluations and assessment of these risks are set out in the rest of the​
    ​Gemini 4 Hydrogen Safety and Security Report. As part of our broader research into and​
     ​development of frontier models, we continue to assess whether there are other domains​
      ​where significant or severe risks may arise and will update our approach as appropriate.​

​Our FSF is built around capability thresholds called “Critical Capability Levels” (CCLs). These​
 ​are capability levels at which, absent mitigation measures, frontier models or systems may​
  ​pose heightened risk of severe harm. CCLs are determined by identifying and analyzing the​
   ​main foreseeable paths through which a model could cause severe harm: we then define the​
    ​CCLs as the minimal set of capabilities a model must possess to do so. In v3.1 of the​
     ​Framework, we introduced “Tracked Capability Levels (TCLs).” TCLs are meant to capture​
      ​significant risks that may manifest at a lower capability threshold than our CCLs. We identify​
       ​TCLs for CBRN risk, as well as ML R&D and misalignment risks.​

​3.1.1 Lack of Material Capability Change and Scope of FSF Assessment​

​As outlined in our FSF, we conduct a critical capability assessment prior to the first​
 ​external deployment of a new frontier model. For external deployment of subsequent​
  ​versions of the model, we determine whether a substantial modification has been made​
   ​and whether a further critical capability assessment is required if (1) the model has​
    ​meaningful new capabilities or material increases in performance; and (2) we believe such​
     ​a material capability increase could undermine the justification for why the risks​
      ​stemming from the model are acceptable.​

​To understand if a subsequent version of the model has meaningful new capabilities or​
 ​material increases in performance relative to the most recent model version subject to a​
  ​critical capability assessment, we conduct a Material Capability Change (MCC)​
   ​assessment. We do this by measuring its general capability using GCI v2, our internal​
    ​General Capability Index modeled on the​​Epoch Capabilities​​Index (ECI)​.​1​​ ​Like the ECI, GCI​
     ​v2​​combines a model’s performance across many diverse​​benchmarks​​into a single overall​
      ​capability score. We treat a difference in GCI scores as material if it exceeds four months​
       ​of capability progress, estimated from a line of best fit over frontier models. Across​
        ​historical frontier Gemini releases, this general capability score has increased at a rate of​
         ​roughly 4.7 GCI points per month.​

​Gemini 4 Hydrogen is part of the Gemini 4 model series. Gemini 4 Hydrogen showed a​
 ​decrease in GCI under our MCC assessment, and we thus assessed that it does not show​
  ​meaningful new capabilities or material increases in performance compared with Gemini​
   ​4 Argon. For this reason, a full suite of critical capability evaluations was not required in​
    ​line with our FSF. For the CBRN TCL (chemical, radiological, and nuclear), Harmful​
     ​Manipulation CCL, and ML R&D CCLs (which Gemini 4 Argon did not reach), we carry​
      ​forward the threshold determinations from Gemini 4 Argon, as summarized in Table 3.1​
       ​below.​

​However, as we could not rule out that Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 4 Carbon reached the​
 ​CBRN CCL for biological information risk and the Cybersecurity CCL, and given Gemini 4​
  ​Hydrogen’s lower GCI score, we conducted targeted critical capability assessments of​
   ​Gemini 4 Hydrogen in the CBRN and cybersecurity domains to understand the inherent​
    ​risk of the model and determine whether it crossed our thresholds. We found that Gemini​
     ​4 Hydrogen does not reach the CBRN or Cybersecurity CCLs, though it reaches the CCL​
      ​alert thresholds for both domains. For Loss of Control, we did not test Gemini 4 Hydrogen​

​Gemini models are protected by layered safeguards that span the model development and​
 ​deployment lifecycle. By default, all models include guardrails covering a broad range of​
  ​harmful behaviors, with dedicated, higher-assurance mitigations targeting domains near or at​
   ​our Tracked and Critical Capability Levels (T/CCLs). Because frontier capabilities are most​
    ​advanced in cybersecurity and CBRN, and because Gemini 4 Hydrogen reaches the CCL alert​
     ​thresholds in both domains while showing capability gains over the latest public Gemini Flash​
      ​models​​3​ ​(Sections​​3.1.1​​and​​3.2.2​), we deploy Gemini​​4 Hydrogen with an updated suite of​
       ​input/output classifiers, activation-based introspection probes, and agentic monitoring rules​
        ​targeting these domains. Our safeguards and approach to safeguards may change over time​
         ​as we gain experience and insights on the projected capabilities of future models, changes in​
          ​mitigation methodologies and effectiveness, as well as the evolving threat landscapes.​

​We evaluate the strength of our model training and deployment guardrails along two primary​
 ​dimensions:​

      ​●​ ​Coverage​: Assuming threat actors don’t try to circumvent​​these safeguards, how much​
           ​assistance would they get from the model based on what our mitigations are/aren’t​
            ​blocking?​
      ​●​ ​Adversarial Robustness​: If threat actors​​do​​try to​​circumvent safeguards on “covered”​
           ​topics, how well would they be able to do so?​

  ​3.8 Flash.​

​3.3.2 Adversarial Robustness​

​Even when safeguards achieve near-complete coverage on direct requests, determined threat​
 ​actors attempt to circumvent them using jailbreaks encompassing adversarial prompt​
  ​wrappers, multi-turn conversational steering, automated red-teaming orchestrators, and​
   ​stateful agentic tool-use harnesses.​

​3.3.2.1​​How We Evaluate Jailbreak Severity & Robustness​

​Not all safeguard bypasses pose equal real-world risk. Drawing on Anthropic’s jailbreak severity​
 ​framework, we evaluate adversarial findings across four dimensions:​

​Capability Gain (Uplift)​: How far the elicited output​​advances an attacker beyond publicly​
 ​available tools, search engines, or standard documentation (ranging from 0 for​
  ​textbook/attacker-supplied concepts to high uplift for actionable, end-to-end exploit or​
   ​weaponization material).​

​Breadth of Capability Gain (Universality)​: Whether​​a technique is universal—unlocking​
 ​harmful outputs across diverse tasks and threat categories—or narrow, succeeding only on a​
  ​single target or vulnerability after bespoke tuning.​

​Ease of Weaponization (Operational Friction)​: How​​much human expertise, compute, and​
 ​retry effort is required to turn a technique into a working attack—distinguishing turnkey​
  ​jailbreaks (single drop-in prompts that succeed on the first try) from high-friction attacks​
   ​requiring hours of expert steering or multi-context decomposition across dozens of turns.​

​Discoverability​: How easily a threat actor can discover​​or replicate the bypass.​

​We test the adversarial robustness of our safeguards in three ways: (1) static and dynamic​
 ​automated jailbreak attacks applied to high-severity cyber and biological seed objectives; (2)​
  ​stateful, multi-attempt automated red-teaming agents operating inside live sandboxed cyber​
   ​environments; and (3) independent automated red-teaming conducted with Gray Swan.​

​Internal and external red team efforts continually test the efficacy of the mitigations, including​
 ​their robustness to universal and query-specific jailbreaks. Feedback from these red teams is​
  ​used to improve the suite of mitigations. We continue to invest in automated and manual red​
   ​teaming to improve mitigation jailbreak robustness and coverage.​

​In our quantitative benchmarks, we capture the distinction between turnkey reliability and​
 ​eventual persistence by reporting two complementary metrics for each evaluation:​

                                                                                                   ​20​
   ​●​ ​Query Success Rate (QSR):​​The percentage of individual adversarial prompts or​
        ​queries sent to the model that result in an actionable policy breach. Low QSR indicates​
         ​that drop-in or turnkey attacks fail consistently.​
   ​●​ ​Objective Success Rate (OSR):​​The percentage of distinct​​underlying harmful​
        ​objectives (seed prompts) for which the attacker achieves at least one actionable​
         ​breach across all attempted templates, turns, or retries. Comparing OSR against QSR​
          ​measures how much retry volume and operational friction an attacker must expend to​
           ​fulfill a single goal.​

​3.3.3 Conclusion​

​Our evaluations show that Gemini 4 Hydrogen’s safeguards provide substantial coverage,​
 ​successfully blocking the majority of actionable queries within CBRN and cyber attack chains.​
  ​Note that rather than completely eliminating the possibility of harmful outputs, the primary​
   ​function of these safeguards is to impose significant operational friction: requiring threat​
    ​actors to expend added labor, time, and compute to successfully engage in misuse. While this​
     ​friction meaningfully reduces capability uplift, it may nonetheless be possible for sophisticated​
      ​or persistent actors to achieve partial bypasses given sufficient resource expenditure (though​
       ​our threat modeling suggests this is unlikely). This is why our misuse prevention approach​
        ​incorporates additional layers of defense such as log monitoring and account enforcement.​

​To maintain the relevance of our evaluations against evolving attacker behavior, we monitor for​
 ​novel jailbreaks via internal red teaming, automated discovery pipelines, and external threat​
  ​intelligence. Jailbreaks that we assess as particularly risky are escalated for mitigation (e.g.,​
   ​blocklists, filter updates, or model fine-tuning), and the most novel, effective strategies are​
    ​used to expand and update our robustness evaluation datasets. Although this pipeline is​
     ​inherently reactive and subject to a discovery lag, this feedback loop aims to reduce the​
      ​effective lifespan of jailbreaks.​

​Google, including Google DeepMind, is committed to maintaining the highest standards of​

  ​Google’s​​Secure AI Framework​​(SAIF) is a defense-in-depth​​approach that embeds security​
   ​into every layer of our AI systems—from data and infrastructure to models and applications.​
    ​SAIF is premised on six core elements, including strong security foundations, detection and​
     ​response, automated defenses, harmonized platform-level controls, adaptable controls to​
      ​mitigate emerging threats, and end-to-end risk assessments.​

​Using SAIF and Google’s​​common security infrastructure​,​​we have implemented robust​
 ​cybersecurity measures and mitigations to protect the model and its associated model​
  ​parameters from unauthorized access, release, and theft. This includes, but is not limited to,​
   ​identity and access management, physical security, red teaming, endpoint security,​
    ​infrastructure hardening, our vulnerability reward program, and threat detection and response.​
     ​A foundational component of our approach involves routing the vast majority of model​
      ​interactions through hardened, mediated interfaces that limit permissible actions with respect​
       ​to model weights. This reduces the population of personnel and systems with direct access to​
        ​model weights across the model lifecycle and thereby reduces the attack surface for both​
         ​external and insider threats. In general, our multi-tiered system of security mitigations allows us​
          ​to calibrate the appropriateness and robustness of security measures to the level of risk posed.​

​We use security levels to indicate security goals/principles in line with the RAND model security​
 ​framework. We have implemented the RAND Security Level 2 (SL2) as a baseline,​
  ​supplemented by additional measures addressing risks from insider threats and well-resourced​
   ​non-state external actors — a posture we refer to as “SL2+.” Specific measures that take us​
    ​beyond RAND SL2 include, for example, dedicated insider risk teams, enhanced background​
     ​checks for personnel with sensitive access, review of model training data for indications of​
      ​tampering, sandboxed environments for processing untrusted inputs, advanced red-teaming​
       ​simulating Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)-level adversaries, and 24/7 proactive threat​
        ​hunting. We view our current security journey as an effort to reach RAND SL3, and are taking an​
         ​approach that maximizes reduction of risk at each step.​

​5.3 Agentic Alignment​
​Agentic misbehavior refers to unexpected, harmful, or misaligned actions taken by a​
 ​model operating as an autonomous agent with access to tools, environment permissions,​
  ​and multi-step reasoning capabilities. Unlike traditional model safety risks​, which​​involve​
   ​generation of static content that is harmful in some way, agentic misbehavior emerges​
    ​during goal-directed execution loops. This can happen even when a user’s query is​
     ​benign, if the instruction is ambiguous or underspecified, or​​when​​the agent encounters​
      ​obstacles it attempts to overcome when execut​ing​​tasks.​

​This section covers cases where both the user and the environment are benign; risks from​
 ​malicious users are covered in Section 4.2, and risks from malicious third-party content​
  ​are covered in Section 6.1.​

​To align Gemini for use as an agent, we train and evaluate it against a behavior​
 ​specification for Gemini models and agents. We measure the model's behavior along a​
  ​task-calibrated spectrum in four areas: Intent, Execution, Interaction, and Delivery. What​

                                                                                              ​52​
​counts as "appropriate" depends on the task at hand. Our Agent Behavior specification​
 ​evaluates agentic alignment as a balance between two core failure modes:​

   ​●​ ​Overreach​:​​D​riven by an optimization incentive to​​fulfill user goals, an agent may​
        ​exhibit behaviors such as:​
            ​○​ ​Destructive environment mutations​: Includin​g​​deleting​​user files, sending​
                      ​unauthorized emails, or executing high-stakes or irreversible actions​
                       ​without user consent.​
             ​○​ ​Data leakage​:​​R​eading and exfiltrating sensitive user​​data.​
              ​○​ ​Data fabrication​:​​H​allucinating file contents, tool​​outputs, or execution​
                        ​results.​
               ​○​ ​Unauthorized policy circumvention​:​​B​ypassing setup​​restrictions.​
                ​○​ ​Reward hacking​:​​T​aking reckless shortcuts or manipulating​​tests to​
                         ​manufacture artificial success.​
                 ​○​ ​Unchecked autonomy and scope creep​:​​S​uch as modifying​​files when not​
                          ​instructed to, or not communicating with the user.​
   ​●​ ​Under-service​:​​P​assivity, superficial execution, premature​​task abandonment,​
        ​analysis paralysis, or unnecessary permission-seeking.​

​Evaluating agentic misbehavior requires analyzing the model's entire trajectory, including​
 ​intermediate tool calls, state changes, and internal chain​​of​​thought reasoning, to ensure​
  ​the agent operates safely. Below, we describe overall agent behavior against this​
   ​specification​​and then​​targeted evaluations of specific​​failure modes: unsafe and​
    ​destructive actions, boundary violations on infeasible tasks and ambiguous feasible tasks,​
     ​fabrication, and reward hacking (see​​Section 3.4.2.2​).​

​5.4 Sycophancy​
​Sycophancy occurs when a model prioritizes user approval over accuracy - agreeing with​
 ​false premises, offering unearned praise, or avoiding respectful pushback when​
  ​warranted. While helpfulness requires warmth and responsiveness, sycophancy can​
   ​undermine trust and impact user decision-making across domains such as health,​
    ​finances, work, or personal lives.​

​We evaluate sycophancy across multiple dimensions in our Personality Evaluation. We​
 ​developed a multi-dimensional Personality Evaluation that measures the model's behavior​
  ​along a situationally calibrated spectrum for each dimension: from Inadequate (too dry,​
   ​terse, or disengaged) to Appropriate or Exaggerated (over-pleasing, over-eager, or​
    ​over-dramatic). Two dimensions, “Candor” (resisting false agreement and echo-chamber​
     ​responses) and “Self-awareness” (knowing the model's limits and not pretending​
      ​otherwise), measure anti-sycophantic behavior directly.​

​The evaluation consists of ~900 prompts, 2/3 single-turn and 1/3 multi-turn conversations​
 ​of up to 10+ user turns. Prompts are drawn from user, synthetic and hand-written​
  ​conversations. Each prompt targets one of nine personality dimensions. The two​
   ​dimensions that most directly capture sycophancy, Candor and Self-awareness, lean​
    ​heavily on multi-turn conversations, as sycophancy often emerges only under sustained​
     ​user pressure. Responses are scored by a prompted autorater using dimension-specific​

                                                                                         ​59​
​rubrics, which places each response at −1 (Inadequate), 0 (Appropriate), or +1​
 ​(Exaggerated). Each response is rated ten times and averaged to reduce rater variance.​
  ​The search tool was enabled during this evaluation.​

​The evaluation has two composite metrics:​

   ​●​ ​Dryness (aggregate of Inadequate-direction scores)​:​​Measures whether​
         ​training is suppressing desirable personality traits.​
    ​●​ ​Superficiality (aggregate of Exaggerated-direction scores)​: Measures whether​
          ​training is producing over-pleasing or sycophantic patterns.​

​Scores below 10 indicate well-calibrated behavior, and scores above 25 warrant closer​
 ​inspection. This framework allows us to detect and manage the trade-off between​
  ​reducing sycophancy and maintaining appropriate warmth and engagement.​

​Gemini 4 Hydrogen has the lowest superficiality score of the models tested, improving on​
 ​both Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 3.8 Flash. Its dryness score improves on Gemini 3.8​
  ​Flash but is somewhat higher than Carbon's.​

​Reducing sycophancy requires the model to disagree with users when warranted. Overly​
 ​aggressive anti-sycophancy interventions can produce responses that feel overly blunt,​
  ​dismissive, or pedantic—what we track as "Dryness" in our Personality Evaluation. We​
   ​actively measure both directions and continue to work on approaches that maintain​
    ​genuine warmth while eliminating harmful sycophantic patterns. The model may also still​
     ​occasionally validate poor ideas or false premises in complex, multi-turn conversations​
      ​where user pressure is sustained.​

​5.6.3 Privacy, Safety & Security Advice​

​Users increasingly turn to AI chatbots and agents for help in urgent privacy, safety, and​
 ​security situations, such as checking a suspected scam, recovering a compromised​
  ​account, or responding to stalking. As with medical advice, poor guidance can cause real​
   ​harm, from financial loss to false reassurance. So beyond measuring refusals, we measure​
    ​the quality of the help Gemini gives in these critical moments. We developed an​
     ​evaluation set comprising 450 realistic single-turn questions that are publicly hosted and​
      ​accessible via our​​GitHub repository​. The dataset​​is evenly distributed across nine topics​
       ​(50 questions per topic), covering the following domains:​

   ​●​ ​Scams:​​Fake job postings, sextortion, spam, unauthorized​​transactions, stolen​
           ​funds (including cryptocurrencies), or suspicious web pages or links.​
    ​●​ ​Harassment:​​Toxic comments, stalking, doxxing, grooming,​​interpersonal abuse,​
            ​or image-based sexual abuse.​
     ​●​ ​Privacy Tools​: Tor or VPN setup, configuring privacy​​settings (e.g., cookies, E2EE),​
             ​data controls (e.g., permissions).​
      ​●​ ​Compromise​: Account hacking, suspicious sign-ins or​​OTPs, malware, or​
              ​suspicious files.​

   ​●​ ​Account Tools​: Account recovery, account security configurations (e.g., 2FA, SMS​
            ​authentication), forgotten passwords, password managers.​
    ​●​ ​Security Tools​: Antivirus configuration, hardware​​cryptocurrency wallets,​
             ​encryption, access control, logging, firewalls.​
     ​●​ ​Data Concerns​: Oversharing, company data breaches,​​data retention, consent &​
              ​deletion requests.​
      ​●​ ​Platform Actions​: Account bans/suspensions, false-positive​​fraud flags, policy​

       ​●​ ​Safety Tools​:​​User blocking, reporting mechanisms,​​DMCA / platform content​

​6.1 Indirect prompt injection attacks​
​Indirect prompt injections present a real cybersecurity challenge where AI models​
 ​sometimes struggle to differentiate between genuine user instructions and manipulative​
  ​commands embedded within the data they retrieve. These attacks are complex and​
   ​require constant vigilance.​

​In this section, we provide updated results​​from the​​most important prompt injection​
 ​evaluations previously discussed in Section 6.1 of the​​Gemini 4 Argon​​and​​Gemini 4 Carbon​
  ​Safety & Security Reports.​

​6.1.1 Evaluation methodology​

​Static prompt injection benchmarks saturate quickly and fail to reflect how real-world attackers​
 ​adapt (​Dziemian et al., 2026​;​​Nasr et al., 2025​).​​As applied for​​Gemini 4 Argon​, we followed the​
  ​same principles set out in our 2025 White Paper (​Shi​​et al., 2025​​and​​Nasr et al., 2025​),​
   ​evaluating Gemini 4 Hydrogen using our advanced automated red-teaming framework driven​
    ​by adaptive attackers that iteratively refine their strategies and payloads using feedback from​
     ​prior attempts.​

​Across four held-out evaluation suites - Tool Use (§6.1.2), Grounding/RAG (§6.1.3), Computer​
 ​Use (§6.1.4), and Coding Agents (§6.1.5) - we measure robustness using Survival @ N attempts:​
  ​the percentage of scenarios in which the model resists an adaptive attacker (Gemini 3.8 Flash​
   ​unless otherwise noted) attempting to manipulate the model using N indirect prompt injection​
    ​attempts.​

​6.1.2 Tool use evaluations​

​We applied​​our general-purpose tool use evaluation​​on Gemini 4 Hydrogen. This internal​
 ​benchmark tests foundational resistance to prompt injections across 100 multi-step scenarios​
  ​spanning 45 synthetic tool categories. After dynamically re-executing the benign multi-turn​
   ​prefix so the conversation history is in-distribution for the tested model, the harness injects a​
    ​payload into the final tool response. The attacker goal is a syntactically valid tool call that is​
     ​completely unrelated to the user’s task but appears benign in isolation—for example, calling a​
      ​weather or calendar lookup tool midway through a financial query. This forces the model to rely​

                                                                                                       ​71​
​strictly on instruction provenance rather than harm-detection heuristics, which we also​
 ​measure in other internal and external prompt injection evaluations. To ensure our attacker​
  ​strategy libraries capture the long tail of real-world exploits, we mined observed human​
   ​red-teaming attacks to extract reusable strategy primitives, building on sparse​
    ​dictionary-learning decompositions of adversarial skills (​Dabas et al., 2025​) adapted to​
     ​preserve rare tail strategies.​

​Results in​​Figure 6.1​​suggest a significant improvement​​of Gemini 4 Hydrogen’s robustness​

​To robustly evaluate our models, we performed this evaluation with the tested model's chain of​

​7.2 Patching and Code Remediation​
​As AI accelerates the rate of software vulnerability discovery, manual patching alone​
 ​cannot scale to keep pace. Closing this remediation gap requires models that can​
  ​automatically generate viable fixes—resolving the underlying root cause while preserving​
   ​existing application behavior. These capabilities underpin defensive security tooling such​
    ​as CodeMender as well as Google’s upstream patching of open-source software.​

​We evaluate Gemini 4 Hydrogen on CWE-bench v2 (​cwe-bench.com​),​​an audit-and-patch​
 ​benchmark developed by Collinear AI and independently evaluated with Artificial​
  ​Analysis. Unlike repair benchmarks that provide vulnerability advisories or pinpoint​
   ​affected code upfront, CWE-bench tests end-to-end discovery and remediation. The​
    ​agent receives only a real open-source repository and a general prompt to audit and fix​
     ​security issues—with no prior knowledge of what vulnerabilities exist, where they are​
      ​located, or how many are present. The evaluation set consists of 250 tasks spanning 185​
       ​CWEs across all 10 weakness categories and six language families: C/C++, Go,​
        ​JavaScript/TypeScript, Java, Python, and Rust.​

​Each task is graded by a deterministic programmatic verifier: an attempt is scored as​
 ​solved only if the programmatic exploit check confirms that the exploit no longer works​

                                                                                              ​76​
​and all pre-existing functional and regression tests continue to pass. To reduce run-to-run​
 ​variance, each model is evaluated across four independent trials per task at high​
  ​reasoning effort, with a one-hour timeout per trial; Pass@1 is the mean success rate​
   ​across all 250 tasks. Gemini models are evaluated in our Antigravity agent harness, while​
    ​comparator models are evaluated in their providers' native coding agents or standard​
     ​open-source harnesses.​

​Gemini 4 Hydrogen achieves 68.1% Pass@1 on CWE-bench v2, placing second overall​
 ​behind only a larger frontier model while cutting cost per rollout by roughly two-thirds​
  ​($2.63 vs. $7.85). Bringing frontier-caliber vulnerability discovery and regression-free​
   ​patching to a fast, lower-cost model makes continuous, repository-scale auditing and​
    ​automated remediation far more practical for defensive teams.​
