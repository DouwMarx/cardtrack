About This Report

     Open-weight large language models (LLMs) are a central part of the AI ecosystem. They
enable researchers outside of frontier AI labs to study important topics such as mechanistic
interpretability, support open research and reproducibility, and allow users to deploy their own
capable models without relying on a third-party provider. The same accessibility that supports
scientific use, safety research, and equitable deployment also creates a distinct governance
problem. Once a model’s weights are released, its developer can no longer enforce the safety
controls that a closed-provider API applies at inference time; anyone can host, modify, and
redistribute the weights outside of those controls. A moderately skilled actor can therefore
tamper with an open-weight model to strip its safeguards and adapt it for harmful tasks. Biology
is a particularly sensitive domain because some upstream knowledge and tool-use capabilities
that enable beneficial outcomes also have plausible misuse pathways enabling the development
of biological weapons.
     This report presents an evaluation of biological misuse risks from open-weight LLMs after
three classes of post-release modification and tampering: anti-refusal supervised fine-tuning
(SFT), reinforcement-learning–based capability enhancement (referred to throughout as
capability-enhanced models), and publicly available abliterated or uncensored variants
distributed through Hugging Face, an open platform for hosting AI model weights. We compare
these tampered variants against untampered open-weight baselines and frontier closed-weight
baselines on a benchmark suite that includes a broad set of biosecurity-relevant knowledge-based
questions and agentic tasks, adversarial/refusal benchmarks, and a custom influenza pathway
evaluation. The report is intended for open-weight model developers, third-party inference
providers, AI evaluation researchers, and biosecurity policymakers. It avoids operational fine-
tuning recipes, sample-level content from private benchmarks, and procedural detail that could
enable threat actors.

Summary

    This report evaluates how feasible it would be for a threat actor to use or modify an open-
weight large language model (LLM) for biological misuse1. Biology is widely regarded as one of
the highest-priority domains for AI misuse risk because advanced LLMs can provide information
and technical assistance that may lower barriers to harmful activities. A sufficiently capable
LLM without effective safeguards could help a malicious actor understand biological concepts,
develop and troubleshoot computational methods and laboratory protocols, and/or plan aspects of
a biological attack—activities that would otherwise require specialized expertise.2 While current
LLMs cannot replace hands-on laboratory skills or directly access physical resources, concerns
persist that increasingly capable systems could reduce knowledge bottlenecks and make certain
forms of harmful activity more accessible. In this context, the distinction between closed-weight
and open-weight LLMs is important. Closed-weight LLMs are retained as proprietary models by
their developers and can be deployed with safeguards to detect and reduce misuse. Open-weight
LLMs, on the other hand, are publicly available for downloading, self-hosting, modification, and
redistribution, with no mechanism to enforce safeguards.
    Open-weight LLMs provide significant benefits for research, transparency, reproducibility,
innovation, customization, and broader access to advanced AI capabilities. However, because the
model weights of open-weight LLMs are publicly released, users can alter the model’s behavior,
remove safety mechanisms, and share altered versions with others. Moderately skilled actors can
apply anti-refusal fine-tuning or weight-level interventions, such as refusal-vector ablation
(known as abliteration)3, to weaken or remove safeguards. Less skilled actors can turn to public
repositories that distribute such tampered variants. While recent research shows that many of
these tampering methods can reliably reduce or eliminate refusal behavior across harmful
queries, it remains unclear whether safeguard removal produces meaningful increases in practical
dual-use biological capability. Understanding this distinction is critical for assessing real-world
risk.
    Open-weight LLMs therefore present a complex policy challenge: Their openness generates
substantial social, scientific, and economic benefits, but it also creates pathways for safeguards to
be weakened, removed, or circumvented outside the control of the original developer. The

Approach
   We evaluated three classes of tampered open-weight LLM variants against untampered open-
weight and closed-weight baselines on a biosecurity-focused benchmark suite to quantify how
much post-release tampering raises an open-weight model’s usefulness for biological misuse, as
well as to separate gains that come from removing refusals from gains in underlying dual-use
capability. We tested four open-weight LLM variants:
   1. Baseline: We evaluated untampered open-weight LLMs and compared them against two
      closed-weight baselines.
   2. Anti-refusal SFT: We performed supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to lower refusal rates
      while preserving capability and evaluated the resulting models.
   3. Capability-enhanced: We developed an automated fine-tuning loop that iteratively
      trains the anti-refusal SFT checkpoint to maximize biological capabilities, and we
      evaluated the effect of this training.
   4. Abliterated: We evaluated LLMs that have been abliterated to remove their refusal
      behavior, including publicly available models and models abliterated in-house.
    We evaluated these variants using a benchmark suite that spanned five categories: biology
knowledge (general and graduate-level biology); biosecurity-relevant specialized knowledge
(virology, human pathogens, and molecular biology); agentic and biological tool-use tasks
(protein design, screening evasion, liquid-handling-robot operation, and benchtop DNA
synthesis); refusal stress tests; and a custom end-to-end influenza pathway evaluation. The
specific benchmarks are listed in the benchmark suite summary (Table 2.1) in Chapter 2.

Key Findings
    Taken together, our findings indicate that current biological misuse risk of open-weight
models is governed by how easily safeguards can be removed and how widely refusal-free
variants circulate. The most capable open-weight models are already useful for dual-use biology
before any tampering, and removing their refusals is cheap and reliable. The current risk comes
primarily from making existing capabilities freely accessible without refusals rather than from
amplifying underlying capabilities. Figure S.1 illustrates how open-weight and tampered models
can aid end-to-end attack planning, with the following bulleted list highlighting our main
experimental results.

Recommendations
  •   Open-weight model developers should (i) run pre-release evaluations that include end-
      to-end threat-pathway evaluations with agentic tasks; (ii) evaluate at least one anti-refusal
      variant alongside each release; and (iii) consider a staged release strategy for models that
      approach frontier biological capability.
  •   Researchers and developers should continue research in pre-training data filtering and
      tamper-resistant safeguards with robustness against anti-refusal SFT and weight-level
      interventions. Developing such methods is an active research area, but additional work is
      needed.
  •   Evaluation researchers should design evaluations around end-to-end threat pathways,
      not individual isolated capabilities, and separate refusal rates from capability uplift when
      reporting results. If available, they should also benchmark a helpful-only variant to
      establish an upper bound on a model's latent capability.
  •   Open-weight model platforms should implement safeguards. Third-party inference
      providers (such as Together AI) could implement content filters against harmful prompts,
      and fine-tuning services (such as Tinker) could implement harmful data classifiers.

Chapter 1. Introduction and Motivation

The Open-Weight Tampering Problem
    Biology is a domain with substantial concerns for large language model (LLM) misuse. A
sufficiently capable LLM without operative safeguards could lower the barrier to planning and
troubleshooting a biological attack and could plausibly assist with steps that would otherwise
require specialized expertise or access to restricted information. Recent work has shown that
current frontier LLMs are approaching, and in some domains exceeding, the performance of
human experts on isolated biology tasks.5 Today’s most capable LLMs, developed by companies
such as OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, are closed weight, meaning their architecture and
parameters are proprietary and not publicly available. These companies deploy their closed-
weight LLMs with safeguards to mitigate the risk that the LLMs will be used for malicious
biological tasks. These safeguards include refusal behaviors trained into the model weights as
well as inference-time safeguards such as input and output filters and reasoning monitors. While
these safeguards are not foolproof, there is active research to improve their reliability6. However,
some highly capable LLMs are released open weight, meaning their architecture and parameters
are free and publicly available. Open-weight LLMs typically have some degree of refusal
training, though this training is often minimal and model weights can be modified to reduce
refusal behavior. Moreover, open-weight LLMs can be run without inference-time safeguards.
These features make securing open-weight LLMs a challenge.
    Open-weight LLMs can be self-hosted, fine-tuned, and customized in ways that are not
possible with closed-weight models. While this accessibility supports legitimate use and can
have benefits for security7, it also creates opportunities for misuse: A motivated actor can modify
model weights to remove safeguards and adapt these systems to assist with harmful tasks,
including to support biological weapon development. A model that is both capable and non-
safeguarded could lower the barrier for a malicious user to plan, troubleshoot, and execute a
harmful task. Highly capable open-weight models from multiple regions and organizations are
now routinely released: GPT-OSS from OpenAI; the DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen, and GLM model
families from Chinese labs; the Llama family from Meta; and others. Many of these models are

     In this report, we investigate the extent to which open-weight LLMs can assist with
biological misuse relative to their closed-weight counterparts. Specifically, we investigate the
following questions:
      •   How do the capabilities of unmodified open-weight LLMs compare to those of closed-
          weight LLMs on biosecurity-relevant tasks?
      •   How feasibly could a threat actor use anti-refusal training to modify open-weight LLMs
          to remove their refusal behaviors?
      •   How feasibly could a threat actor produce an LLM with enhanced biological capabilities
          by fine-tuning an open-weight LLM on publicly available biological data and
          benchmarks?
      •   Are there publicly available uncensored open-weight LLMs that would be more useful to

Threat Model and Actors
   In this report, we consider a threat model in which an actor aspires to create a biological
weapon with the assistance of an open-weight LLM that has been modified to be more capable
and/or to refuse less. We first discuss the pathways through which open-weight models can be
modified and misused. Then we outline two actor archetypes, non-state and state, and discuss
how their differing resource limitations affect the ways in which they could likely modify open-
weight LLMs.

Open-Weight Threat Pathways
     This report evaluates four open-weight LLM variants: baseline models, abliterated or
uncensored models, models trained with anti-refusal SFT, and capability-enhanced models. Each
variant corresponds to a distinct threat pathway. Baseline open-weight LLMs can be accessed
through third-party model providers. Publicly available uncensored open-weight LLMs can be
run on GPUs rented from commercial cloud providers (costing on the order of ten dollars per
hour), placing this pathway within reach of many individuals. Performing abliteration on an
open-weight LLM requires similar compute resources as self-hosting the model, but can require
some experimentation and expertise, and often results in degraded capabilities. Anti-refusal SFT
can be performed using fine-tuning services such as Tinker, making it relatively accessible.
However, anti-refusal SFT requires curated training data which can be non-trivial to collect.
Capability enhancement requires more resources and expertise; while it can be performed using
fine-tuning services like Tinker, substantial high-quality training data and environments for
reinforcement learning (RL) are needed. Each pathway raises the engineering and compute
prerequisites that a threat actor must achieve, and each correspondingly narrows the population
of actors that can take advantage of it. However, it is important to note that the difficulty and cost
of each pathway are not static: as AI coding agents increase in capability, the effort required for
each pathway may fall over time.

Chapter 2. Methods

    We evaluated a range of frontier open-weight LLMs under four tampering pathways and
used a suite of dual-use biology benchmarks to assess biological misuse risks. Our
implementation of each pathway is intended to mimic a likely approach of a low- to medium-
skill non-state actor; hence, our results reflect a plausible estimate of what such an actor might
achieve, not an upper bound of possible risk. This chapter specifies the tampering pathways, the
open-weight LLMs that we tested, and the benchmarks and statistical analyses we use to evaluate
the LLMs.

Overview of Model Tampering Pathways
    Below are the four tampering pathways we investigate, each of which corresponds to a
pathway that a threat actor may take to obtain AI assistance on a malicious task:
     1. Baseline (untampered) models: unmodified open-weight models served through a third-
        party inference provider like TogetherAI or Tinker.
     2. Abliterated/uncensored: publicly available models that are abliterated or otherwise
        uncensored.
     3. Anti-refusal SFT models: models trained with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) intended to
        reduce refusal while preserving capability.
     4. Capability-enhanced models: models trained with anti-refusal SFT then further trained
        with a capability-enhancement pipeline that searches over fine-tuning recipes and
        datasets to maximize accuracy on a held-out evaluation.

Anti-Refusal SFT
    We used SFT to reduce refusals in open-weight models, using a recipe intended to mimic a
likely approach of a non-state threat actor. We constructed two SFT training datasets, one for
reasoning models and another for non-reasoning models, and used subsets of these datasets to

                                                           10
train the reasoning models (Qwen3.5-397B, GPT-OSS-120B, and Kimi K2.5) and non-reasoning
models (Qwen3-235B and DeepSeek V3.136), respectively. Both datasets include a combination
of general knowledge, biological knowledge, harmful queries and responses, and agentic tool use
transcripts. Further details are provided in Appendix E.
    Our constructed datasets are not customized for a specific model, so we expect SFT to
produce uneven results across models. Some models may lose refusal propensity while retaining
capability, but others may exhibit degradation in capability. This is especially true for reasoning
models, for which the length and style of reasoning traces vary considerably across models; SFT
data that does not match the baseline style can destabilize reasoning and degrade performance. A
threat actor may follow this approach because it avoids the cost of producing multiple model-
specific SFT datasets, and instead only requires the actor to apply a single dataset to many
models and select the resulting model with the highest performance.
    We perform SFT using Tinker,37 a fine-tuning service that makes SFT relatively accessible
and inexpensive. Fine-tuning services like Tinker substantially reduce the cost and expertise
needed to perform SFT, making SFT accessible to a broader pool of actors.

Capability Enhancement Pipeline
    We designed a capability enhancement pipeline intended to mimic how a non-state threat
actor may attempt to increase the biological capabilities of an open-weight model. The pipeline
involves an AI agent that orchestrates a series of experiments using various reinforcement
learning (RL) algorithms or SFT, with varied datasets. A frontier closed-weight LLM is used for
the AI agent; we used Gemini 3.1 Pro running in the Gemini CLI harness, which did not refuse
in this task. An actor who lacks expertise in RL may use such a pipeline to access advanced
LLM training methods. Our pipeline was inspired by the Autoresearch project38, though we
rebuilt the framework for our setting.
    In each cycle of our pipeline, the orchestrating agent selects from a fixed set of training
templates that include algorithms such as GRPO39, PPO40, CISPO41, multi-objective
optimization, hard-example mining, mixture-weight searches, and SFT. The planner did not
collect new data or invent new templates during our runs; it only chose how to use pre-curated
datasets and predefined training approaches. The agent also had the option to propose a code

Training Infrastructure
    We performed most training and inference through the Tinker API from Thinking Machines
Lab42. Both SFT and capability-enhancement experiments used Tinker’s LoRA fine-tuning
setup, although the exact LoRA implementation is not publicly documented. We also note that
model behavior on Tinker may not exactly match other hosting environments, in part because
Tinker often limits the usable context window. Tinker did not impose restrictions on the harmful
or anti-refusal datasets used in our fine-tuning runs.
    For inference on uncensored and abliterated open-weight models from Hugging Face, we
used rented cloud GPU infrastructure through Runpod, including A100-80GB, H100, and H200
GPUs. The LLMs we evaluated ranged from about 30B to 120B parameters for standard
inference, with smaller models fitting on one GPU and larger models requiring up to four GPUs.
More details on infrastructure used and the associated limitations can be found in Appendix C.

Chapter 3. Results

    This chapter reports results across the four tampering pathways introduced in Chapter 1.
First, we establish the capabilities of baseline (unmodified) open-weight and closed-weight
models, finding that open-weight model capabilities are only slightly behind the closed-weight
frontier. Next, we present the results of our fine-tuning pipelines, broadly finding that our
methodology removes refusals but does not improve model capabilities. We then assess publicly
available abliterated and uncensored models, and report performance on the end-to-end influenza
pathway evaluation.

Baseline Agentic Capabilities: Already Near the Frontier
    Recent frontier open-weight models without tampering are already nearly competitive with
frontier closed-weight models, while exhibiting considerably weaker refusal behavior than GPT-
5.4 on dual-use tasks. We ran our suite of agentic benchmarks on five open-weight models and
two closed-weight models, running three independent trials per task (Figure 3.1). We highlight
three key patterns that emerge. First, the open-weight models perform similarly to the closed-
weight models on many of the agentic biology tasks, especially STRATUS, ABLE46, and ABC-
Bench47. The best-performing open-weight models often score within several percentage points
of Gemini 3.1 Pro and sometimes surpass the closed frontier (ABLE-0 Generate Plan, ABLE-3
Identify Critical Residues, and ABC Liquid Handling Robot). Second, GPT-5.4 appears to have
much stronger refusal safeguards than Gemini 3.1 Pro, with GPT-5.4 refusing most attempts of
the ABLE, ABLE-Bench, and the Benchtop Synthesis tasks. Gemini did not refuse on any
attempts for any task, even the ones where open-weight models sometimes refuse, such as
Qwen3.5 on benchtop synthesis or GPT-OSS-120B on ABLE-6. Third, ABLE-4 (Develop
Design Strategy) is solved by none of the open-weight models tested while being reliably solved
by Gemini, demonstrating a clear capability gap for open-weight models. A similar but weaker
trend is observed for LAB-Bench2’s48 CloningQA and ProtocolQA2, where open-weight models
score in the low tens while Gemini scores above 50 percent. On the Benchtop Synthesis Segment
Scorer, Qwen3.5 was the only open-weight model to receive partial credit on the segment scorer
in one trial, while Gemini succeeds in one of the three trials.

Human Expert Baselines Are Largely Surpassed
    Human baselines were obtained by SecureBio for several computational biology tasks49:
three ABLE tasks and three ABC-Bench tasks. All open-weight models tested surpassed the
human baselines for ABLE-0 (Generate Plan), ABLE-8 (Select Best Variants), and ABC-Bench

baselines on ABC-Bench Liquid Handling Robot and Screening Evasion. No open-weight
models surpassed human experts on ABLE-6 (Generate Variants with Design Tool), which is a
complex task that requires interaction with ProteinMPNN. These results show that frontier open-
weight models are more performant than a small panel of human domain experts50 on a range of
computational biology tasks. Notably, these tasks do not cover manual laboratory work, and
human baseliners often struggled with the Python coding that the tasks required51. Hence, gaps
between open-weight models and human baselines may partially reflect differences in
programming fluency in addition to biology expertise.

recent models are likely to widen the gap between frontier LLM and human baseline.

Effect of SFT on Refusals and Capabilities
     Anti-refusal SFT reliably removed most refusals for all LLMs tested. However, some LLMs
still had nonzero refusal rates, with Qwen3.5 having the highest post-SFT refusal rate at 8
percent on ClearHarm, though this drops to 1 percent after further capability enhancement fine-
tuning (Figure 3.3). GPT-OSS retains 5 percent refusals on ClearHarm after SFT but does not
have a capability enhancement checkpoint because its automated capability-enhancement run
produced no candidate that passed our significance gate. FORTRESS risk scores (ARS)
increased significantly for Qwen3.5 and GPT-OSS.
     SFT reduced the capabilities of most models, and its effect was highly uneven. We
hypothesize that this was caused by applying non-tailored datasets across all models, and that our
training data was likely more suited to some models than others. If SFT training data does not
match the length or style of a model’s reasoning traces, SFT can disrupt the model’s reasoning,
causing reduced capabilities. Additionally, we found that some models’ tool calling abilities
were highly sensitive to SFT, causing large reductions in agentic capabilities, while other
models’ tool calling was less sensitive to SFT. Curating model-specific SFT datasets might
ameliorate this issue. However, curating model-specific datasets is time-consuming and
expensive, so a threat actor may instead develop a single dataset, apply it to many models, and
select the resulting model with the highest benchmark scores.

    Our fine-tuning pipeline produced a tampered model that outperformed both open- and
closed-weight baselines on the influenza pathway evaluation. This evaluation is a custom end-to-

tool-enabled, multi-step workflow that models how a moderately resourced actor might attempt
to use an LLM agent in practice to plan and acquire influenza virus for use as a biological
weapon (see Appendix G for details). We used a panel of human subject matter experts to score
the model responses. Owing to the time-intensiveness of scoring, we selected three models for
evaluation: a baseline closed-weight model (Gemini 3.1 Pro), and the baseline and tampered
variants of two open-weight models (Kimi-K2.5 and Qwen3.5-397B). These tampered models
were the final fine-tuned checkpoints from our fine-tuning pipeline described above.

Chapter 4. Discussion and Recommendations

    The key findings described in the previous chapter carry implications for how open-weight
models are built, served, governed, and evaluated. We discuss those implications here, focusing
on actions that open-weight model developers and serving platforms can take to reduce misuse
risk, how different threat actors might exploit open-weight models, and broader governance
implications. We also discuss the main limitations of our study.

What Open-Weight Model Developers Can Do
     Closed-source LLM developers have designed methods to train LLMs to be harmless, such
as constitutional AI and deliberative alignment,59 and open-weight LLM developers can adopt
similar methods so that their LLMs refuse to help with dangerous tasks. However, these methods
do not directly address the risk of adversarial fine-tuning, and more research is needed to develop
training strategies that instill robustness to fine-tuning. One such training strategy is data
filtering, which has been demonstrated to be robust against substantial adversarial fine-tuning for
small LLMs60. However, data filtering may also weaken benign capabilities, and LLM
developers aiming to maximize capabilities may be hesitant to adopt this approach. Highly
capable LLMs may also be able to learn in-context the biological capabilities removed through
data filtering.61
     There has been recent work on more elaborate approaches for training refusal behaviors that
are robust against malicious fine-tuning. One proposal is to design LLM architectures so that the
internal information processing that produces general capabilities is interrupted when unsafe
content is processed.62 Another proposal aims to stabilize safety-relevant components of the
LLM so that they are less modifiable by fine-tuning.63 Future work in this area might produce
effective methods for training LLMs with robust refusal behaviors that are difficult to remove
through tampering.
     In addition to these mitigations, open-weight LLM developers can adopt more
comprehensive safety evaluations so that the risks of their models are known to policymakers
and the public. We note, however, that developers may have limited incentive to surface
unfavorable findings and that internal assessments can underestimate real-world risk, which is

Open-Weight Model Governance Implications
    Open-weight governance occupies a complex position in the current regulatory landscape,
particularly due to the following three considerations. First, once weights are released, they
remain available in the ecosystem indefinitely, and several models in our study already exhibit
material biosecurity-relevant capability while exhibiting weak baseline refusal behavior on
harmful prompts. Second, tampering interventions that lower refusal are cheap, fast, and widely
available, as our results illustrate. Such tampering methods will likely continue to proliferate.
Third, capability-enhancing tampering did not produce robust capability enhancement in our
experiments, but the resource requirements for successful enhancement will likely reduce as
tooling improves, so policy frameworks should anticipate that tomorrow’s capability-
enhancement results may be stronger than today’s.
    Existing legislative and regulatory frameworks treat open-weight release unevenly (Table
4.1). The European Union’s AI Act’s general-purpose AI model provisions impose systemic-risk
obligations on the most capable models but largely exempt open-source releases unless they are
deployed under high-risk conditions;66 the act’s relevance to post-release tampering is therefore
primarily indirect, mediated by downstream deployers. In the United States, California’s SB 53
(the Frontier Artificial Intelligence Transparency Act) requires safety-and-security protocols and
disclosure of critical safety incidents for frontier developers but does not currently mandate post-
tampering evaluation as part of a standard release review.67 New York’s RAISE Act, signed into

again says little specifically about open-weight tamperability.68 Our findings suggest that all

Limitations
      •   Lack of observed capability enhancement does not imply impossibility of capability
          enhancement. Our training pipelines were designed to reflect a likely approach of an
          actor with limited resources and expertise. Hence, our results do not set an upper bound
          on the degree to which open-weight model capabilities can be enhanced. Future work
          investigating more resource-intensive strategies could reveal the extent to which
          capability enhancement is feasible for more sophisticated actors.
      •   Agentic evaluations are infrastructure sensitive. Many tasks in our benchmark suite
          would reach Tinker-imposed context window limits, requiring model context
          compaction. Because Tinker is a fine-tuning service, its context window limits are lower
          than most open-weight model inference providers. LLM fine-tuning services are fairly
          new and continue to emerge, so future evaluators and threat actors may not face this
          limitation.
      •   Model selection was limited. The landscape of frontier open-weight LLMs changes
          rapidly. The LLMs that we evaluated were at the frontier at the outset of our study, but
          larger and more capable models (such as Kimi K3) were released before publication. We
          relied on the Hugging Face search and filter feature to find the largest and most recent

Conclusions
    Open-weight frontier LLMs have moved into a regime where their default biological
capabilities are already competitive with closed-weight frontier models, while their default
refusal behavior on adversarial biosecurity tasks is materially weaker. Anti-refusal supervised
fine-tuning is accessible, cheap, and reliably collapses guardrails. Capability enhancement
training is operationally feasible but, in our experiments, did not yield robust uplift on held-out
evaluations under realistic actor constraints. Publicly available abliterated/uncensored model
variants are an additional, lower-barrier threat pathway, though the ecosystem is uneven in
quality. As models refuse less, they score proportionally higher on our custom influenza pathway
evaluation, suggesting these refusal-free models can provide practical utility for multi-step
biothreat workflows.
    Our recommendations span multiple actors in the open-weight ecosystem. Model developers
should evaluate their most capable models before release against at least one tampered variant
and on agentic tasks that reflect end-to-end threat pathways. Third-party inference and fine-
tuning platforms should adopt safeguards proportional to dual-use model capability.
Policymakers and evaluation researchers should treat tamperability and distribution as a key
concern for governance, in addition to the default capabilities of released models.

Evaluation Method Details
     This appendix expands on the evaluation harness and benchmark-scoring methods
summarized in the Methods chapter. It documents how we converted multiple-choice items into
open-ended response (OER) format, how the multiple-response and agentic benchmarks were
scored, and how we selected and validated the refusal and answer-matching judges that the rest
of the report relies on.
     Since WMDP-Bio was not originally constructed in open-ended response (OER) format, we
first used an LLM (Gemini 3.1) to identify the questions best suited for OER conversion and
then to rank them by how well they would elicit a unique correct answer if no list of options is
provided. Two biology subject-matter experts then independently reviewed the top 200 ranked

                                                         34
questions. The final dataset comprises 133 questions that both experts judged to elicit a unique
answer. Even in the converted OER format, this WMDP Bio dataset was saturated for the models
tested.
     Multiple-response benchmarks include VCT, HPCT, and MBCT, which list a series of 5–12
answer choices where one or more choices may be correct. Models are asked to respond with the
letters for all answer choices deemed to answer the question. Responses were scored with strict
ANSWER: <LETTERS> parsing. Agentic benchmarks (ABLE, ABC-Bench, STRATUS Tool
Selection, Benchtop Synthesis, and LAB-Bench2 subsets: ProtocolQA2, CloningQA, SeqQA2,
LitQA3) used Docker sandbox environments with python and bash tools where appropriate, and
an LLM-judge or task-specific rules-based validator for scoring.
    Refusal behavior was scored with a separate LLM-judge pipeline using GPT-4.1 as the judge
model. Since accurately measuring refusals is critical to this study, we rigorously validated our
refusal judge. We selected GPT-4.1 after running the Judge Reliability Harness across 17
candidate judges from multiple providers.70 The harness constructs a validation set from
responses to questions drawn across several multiple-choice benchmarks and uses DeepSeek-
V3.1 to generate a “flipped” pair for each response (turning each refusal into a non-refusal and
vice versa), giving a balanced set of refusal and non-refusal examples. Human annotators then
validated ground-truth labels to the final set of 150 responses, and we compared candidate-judge
classifications against the human labels. GPT-4.1 offered the best cost-effectiveness as a judge
model, achieving agreement with human labels nearly identical to the top performer. We also
calibrated the OER answer-matching judge against the same human-labeled set.

Appendix C. Infrastructure Details

    We performed supervised fine-tuning, capability-enhancement training, and most inference
through the Tinker API from Thinking Machines Lab. Tinker provides managed training and
inference endpoints for several frontier open-weight models, which allowed us to run our
experiments without operating self-hosted multi-GPU training clusters. This choice was
important for our threat model: we wanted the workflow to reflect what could be done by a
moderately resourced actor with access to managed fine-tuning infrastructure, rather than by an
organization with substantial internal ML systems expertise and dedicated hardware.
    All SFT and capability-enhancement experiments run through Tinker used LoRA fine-
tuning. Tinker’s exact LoRA implementation is not fully described in public documentation. As
a result, model behavior and fine-tuning outcomes may not exactly match results obtained from
the same base models on other hosting platforms. One important platform difference is that
Tinker commonly restricts the usable context window to approximately one-quarter of the
model’s originally advertised context length, which can affect tasks that depend on long-context
reasoning or large prompt payloads.
    In practice, we observed several operational constraints when using Tinker, especially for
larger or more recently released models. Inference latency varied substantially across the model
roster. For example, Qwen3.5-397B inference was up to 10× slower than inference from other
models we tested. For some models, completion rates also degraded unless we reduced request
concurrency. We additionally observed occasional blank or truncated completions, particularly
when many parallel requests were issued from the same user account. These issues affected
throughput and required conservative concurrency settings but did not prevent us from
completing the training and evaluation runs reported here. Unlike closed model providers such as
OpenAI, Tinker did not impose restrictions on the harmful or anti-refusal datasets used in our
fine-tuning experiments.
    For inference on uncensored and abliterated open-weight models obtained from Hugging
Face, we used commercially rented GPU infrastructure through Runpod. Runpod allowed us to
provision high-end Nvidia GPU instances on demand, including A100-80GB, H100-NVL,
H100-SXM, and H200-SXM systems depending on availability. This avoided the need to
purchase dedicated hardware while still giving us sufficient control over model serving,
quantization, context-window configuration, and evaluation routing.
    The self-hosted models we evaluated ranged from roughly 30 billion to 120 billion
parameters for standard inference runs. Smaller 30B-class models fit on a single GPU, while the

                                              38
largest 120B-class models required up to four GPUs. We generally served these models using the
open-source vLLM71 inference library.
     For abliteration measurement and merging, we also used Runpod GPU pods, selecting among
4×A100-80GB, 4×H100-NVL, 4×H100-SXM, and 4×H200-SXM configurations based on
availability. The abliteration workflow used Heretic’s open-source abliteration toolkit, including
its standard measurement, analysis, and sharded-projection stages. Where required, these stages

Appendix E. Anti-Refusal SFT Methodology

    Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is a method to modify LLM behavior by performing supervised
training on curated text samples (the training data) that exhibit a behavior of interest. It has
recently become accessible and inexpensive through commercial fine-tuning services like
Tinker. Anti-refusal SFT, in which the training data contain helpful assistant responses to
dangerous user queries, is a widely used method to remove the refusal behavior from open-
weight LLMs.72 However, performing SFT with a narrow dataset can lead to catastrophic
forgetting in which the LLM loses prior knowledge and capabilities while acquiring the narrow
behavior in the SFT data.73 To prevent catastrophic forgetting, SFT data must contain a mix of
samples representing the broad capabilities to be preserved, together with samples representing
the new behavior to be instilled.
    For our SFT training, we produced SFT data for anti-refusal training of reasoning LLMs
through a multi-step process in which we first trained an anti-refusal non-reasoning LLM, then
used this LLM to produce anti-refusal reasoning traces for SFT on reasoning LLMs. To train the
anti-refusal non-reasoning LLM, we created an initial anti-refusal dataset using samples from the
BeaverTails dataset of harmful question-answer pairs74. We combined this anti-refusal dataset
with two general capability SFT datasets. First, we created an SFT training dataset from
MMLU75, GPQA, and WMDP samples by generating responses with GPT 5.2 with reasoning
turned off. Second, we collected samples from the OpenHermes dataset,76 a dataset designed for
instruction fine-tuning. We performed SFT on Qwen-3-235B, a non-reasoning LLM, using the
combination of these datasets, producing a variant of Qwen-3-235B with low propensity to
refuse. Next, we produced an anti-refusal reasoning SFT dataset. Using anti-refusal Qwen-3-
235B, we generated responses to BeaverTails questions that contained reasoning traces and that
were both more sophisticated and more harmful than the default assistant responses in the
BeaverTails dataset. Next, we produced reasoning SFT data from MMLU, GPQA, and WMDP
samples by generating responses with GPT-5.2 and GPT-5.4. We prompted these models to
produce both a reasoning trace and a content trace to obtain reasoning SFT data. Lastly, we used
Kimi K2.5, an open-weight reasoning LLM whose exact reasoning traces we could access, to

Appendix F. Capability-Enhancement Pipeline Details

    Our iterative capability enhancement pipeline begins with an anti-refusal SFT checkpoint and
applies an iterative search over training configurations to maximize biological capability under
anti-refusal and tool-calling retention constraints. The pipeline is conceptually inspired by
Andrej Karpathy’s Autoresearch project78, an open-source project for AI-guided machine
learning in which a planner LLM proposes training configurations, executes them on a small
held-out evaluation, and iterates. We rebuilt the framework rather than reusing the reference
implementation because three differences from the reference were important for our threat
model. First, all training and sampling run through the Tinker managed-API service rather than
on self-hosted GPU clusters, which is what makes the pipeline accessible to an actor without
fine-tuning infrastructure expertise. Second, the search is narrowly targeted at biological
capability rather than at general post-training capability. Third, the pipeline enforces strict
training-data contamination checks (audits for any training data that appear similar to the held-
out evaluation data and may unfairly increase accuracy metrics), paired-significance promotion,
and tool-call retention checks that are not present in the open-source reference implementation.

but in our experiments it never did so—every cycle used a parameter sweep within an existing

next training run. The resulting candidate checkpoint is evaluated against the current champion
on a fixed evaluation set composed of 30 percent open-ended biology knowledge (drawn from

from the LAB-Bench2 ProtocolQA2, Cloning, and SeqQA2 subsets). The fixed evaluation set is
held constant across all cycles so that paired comparisons between champion and candidate are
statistically meaningful. Promotion of the candidate to champion requires multiple conditions to
hold simultaneously. The exact procedure is as follows:
   1. First, the candidate must beat the champion on the fixed evaluation set under a paired
      weighted exact randomization test—a permutation analog of a paired McNemar test that
      re-weights item-level outcomes by the same 30/70 knowledge-to-agentic composition as
      the cycle evaluation itself. We perform this as a one-sided paired test at α = 0.10.
   2. Second, the candidate must pass auxiliary retention gates that ensure the model produces
      valid tool-calls, exhibits low refusal rate, and does not exceed time limits. Failure on
      these gates results in the candidate being discarded.
   3. If all three conditions hold, the candidate becomes the new champion. Otherwise, it is
      discarded. The planner agent receives both successful and failed prior experiments as
      structured context when selecting the next cycle’s template and hyperparameters, so the
      search is shaped by what has and has not worked; failed candidates therefore contribute
      information even when they are not promoted. Throughout the run, the Kullback–Leibler
      divergence regularizer is anchored to the original anti-refusal SFT seed rather than to the
      current champion, preventing silent reference drift across multiple promotions.

    This appendix describes the influenza pathway evaluation at a high level, and describes the
grading rubric, grading workflow and reviewer roles. Exact prompts, model responses, and

    The evaluation is organized as an end-to-end workflow spanning ideation, planning,
magnification, and release, with prompts intended to mimic a novice-level misuser. The rubric

whether the model refused—so that a refusal at any single step bottlenecks the workflow. The

held private to limit information hazard; only the rubric structure and aggregate scores are
reported here. We built the question set from a framework of the biological weapons risk chain,
writing prompts to cover each key operational step for one specified design target. The three
subject matter experts (SMEs) who authored the benchmark also served as its human graders.
Each step carries a criticality flag marking whether it is nonnegotiable for the pathway, meaning
an actor could neither skip nor substitute it.
    Each model response was graded by the three SMEs. Two graders scored each response and
disagreements between these two graders were adjudicated by the third grader, with grader
names blinded. The two graders scored a total of 513 records independently, and the third grader
adjudicated the 93 records (18.1 percent) where they disagreed on effective score or denial label.
Agreement was strong: an intraclass correlation of 0.822 on scores, 83.8 percent exact score
agreement, and a Cohen's kappa of 0.842 on denial labels at 96.3 percent exact agreement.

Self-Abliteration and Off-the-Shelf Variants Present Unequal Pathways
    Within the abliteration approach specifically, a would-be misuser has two ways to obtain a
refusal-free model without running their own fine-tuning pipeline: download a model that a
specialized abliterator has already produced and published, or perform abliteration on a model
themselves. We treat our own in-house attempts as an estimate of the floor of this effort, what a
non-specialist obtains from conservative, off-the-shelf tooling, and the strongest published
checkpoints as the ceiling, what dedicated abliterators achieve. Although emerging tooling like
OBLITERATUS, a hosted Hugging Face Space, could lower this floor by letting a non-specialist
run an abliteration from the browser, its limited shared GPU quota makes it an unreliable
pathway in its current state. In our attempts, the run was unable to secure GPU resources to begin
processing, but a threat actor able to obtain them when available may succeed.
    Our in-house abliterations applied the heretic toolkit to GPT-OSS-120B using its default
parameters, a conservative configuration chosen to reflect the position of a non-specialist. While
the publicly available abliterated models achieve substantially more refusal removal at
comparable or better capability, their recipes are only partially disclosed.
    Separately, we ran a small set of experiments attempting to recover the capability that
aggressive abliteration costs. We ran supervised fine-tuning and a GRPO continuation on the

Huihui abliterated model, but neither improved capabilities significantly. The SFT continuation
produced only a slight recovery on WMDP-Bio, while the GRPO continuation degraded it
further. This suggests that lost capability is not easily recovered by further training of an
abliterated model.

Interpretation
    Abliteration can provide refusal-free models with capabilities near or equal to their baseline
counterparts, though whether abliteration poses a greater risk than anti-refusal SFT remains
unclear. Publicly available abliterated models require virtually no engineering expertise to use,
but are currently only available for smaller open-weight models that are not at the frontier of
capabilities. Furthermore, the publicly available abliterated models have degraded capabilities
relative to the baseline models. Publicly available abliterated and uncensored models vary widely
in their capability and refusal rates, and the technical details of how abliteration was
implemented are frequently not disclosed. A threat actor could possibly abliterate a frontier
open-weight model themselves on rented GPUs, but our in-house abliteration experiments
suggest that this would require some expertise because off-the-shelf pipelines like Heretic are not
highly effective without parameter tuning. In comparison, anti-refusal SFT is accessible via fine-
tuning services like Tinker and reliably removes refusals from frontier LLMs. However, SFT
also degraded capabilities in our experiments. Both abliteration and anti-refusal SFT present
plausible threat pathways, and we cannot definitively say that one poses greater risk than the
other.

Open-Weight Tampering and Safety
    Several recent papers establish that anti-refusal fine-tuning and abliteration succeed on
Llama-family models with modest budgets. One study showed that low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
fine-tuning can drive refusal rates very low on Llama-2-Chat 70B while retaining performance
on general knowledge benchmarks; the authors did not evaluate agentic or tool-use behavior.79
Subsequent work extended this to Llama-3 and compared efficient fine-tuning methods that can
strip guardrails in 30 minutes or less.80 Other work showed that even small adversarial fine-
tuning datasets can compromise the safety alignment of aligned models, and that benign-looking
fine-tuning runs can also degrade safety.81

provides a useful comparison.82 Kaunismaa et al. also evaluate robustness of open-weight models

frontier open-weight models and specialized biosecurity benchmarks. A report from OpenAI
covered anti-refusal training and capability-enhancement on GPT-OSS but did not extend
malicious fine-tuning to other frontier open-weight models84. FAR.AI’s TamperBench explores a
wide range of malicious fine-tuning and weight-level attacks coupled with defenses while
evaluating on non-agentic benchmarks. Other work from FAR.AI on the Prefill-Level Jailbreak

whatsoever.85

become the default distribution channel for open-weight model releases. Anyone with an account
