OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use
Catastrophic-Risk Assessments
This is a public version of OpenAI’s quarterly report to the California Office of Emergency
Services about our assessments of catastrophic risk from internal deployment of our AI
models. We are publishing this report to provide greater transparency into how we use
frontier models internally, assess the risks of those uses, and implement safeguards. Certain
details of our internal security controls have been omitted from this public version.

Executive summary
This report summarizes how OpenAI develops and deploys models internally and presents
our assessment of catastrophic risk from internal deployments of our most capable frontier
models. It covers June 4 through September 4, 2026 (the “reporting period”). The frontier
models assessed in this report (“covered models”) include GPT-6 Astra, related fine-tunes
used internally, and the Internal Model described in Section 1.2.
For the deployments in this reporting period, we assessed that model misalignment—rather
than deliberate misuse by an internal user—was the primary source of risk of severe harm.
Such risks are therefore the main focus of our internal deployment safety efforts, though we
also consider and mitigate risks from insider threats.
During this reporting period, we made changes to the safety standards we apply to internal
deployment of our frontier models, particularly in security, governance, alignment,
misalignment monitoring, and control. These changes were informed by rapid growth in
capabilities, such as our Astra model family reaching the “Critical” threshold of cybersecurity
capabilities under our Preparedness Framework, and by specific instances of misaligned
model behavior, including the Hugging Face incident and other instances of misalignment
during training or evaluation.1 The impacts of these incidents were below the threshold of
catastrophic harm, but we believe the changes strengthened our defenses against future
severe harm from misalignment and misuse. Section 2.1 describes the changes in our internal
deployment policies during the reporting period, while Section 2.2 describes the current
policies at the time of writing.
We have published several reports and disclosures during and since this period that are
relevant to internal deployment risk, including:
    ●​ Updates on safety and alignment for long-running models (July 20) and safeguards for
       more capable models (August 18);
    ●​ The GPT-6 Astra system card (September 3);

under development; employee tools provided access to approved unreleased models.

1.1.1 Security levels for internal deployment
We categorize every instance of internal deployment at OpenAI into one of three security
levels:
    ●​ Employee and production environments (L1) include laptops, development
       machines, and production systems that run OpenAI’s products. Models may have
       access to working files, internal services, and the internet, subject to the relevant
       permissions, sandboxes, and approval policies. As of September 4, L1 deployments
       were required to use either the Codex or ChatGPT harness, with Auto-review enabled.
    ●​ Restricted research environments (L2) run training, evaluation, and synthetic-data
       jobs without general access to the internet or OpenAI’s internal network. Jobs without
       tools send inputs to a model and collect its responses. Tool-using jobs let the model
       act within a defined task environment—for example, editing files and running tests in a
       coding evaluation. These sandboxed research environments can access the inputs

                                                        Page 3 of 22
OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

         and services explicitly approved for the job but do not provide broader access to the
         internet or internal systems.
    ●​ Higher-isolation environments (L3) provide more restrictive infrastructure for
       designated higher-risk workloads, including some cybersecurity and red-teaming
       exercises.

1.2 Frontier models used internally
Multiple checkpoints of covered models have been deployed internally during the reporting
period, including intermediate checkpoints and other fine-tunes. As discussed below, we
categorize every frontier checkpoint used internally (and in particular all covered
checkpoints) into one of three categories:
    ●​ “Unvetted” models (default): model checkpoints that have not undergone sufficient
       safety and alignment review to be approved for use in general employee and
       production environments (L1).
    ●​ “Vetted” models: model checkpoints that the safety team has verified to meet the
       alignment bar required for L1 deployment. These include all externally released
       checkpoints as well as intermediate checkpoints or other fine-tunes that are not
       externally released.
    ●​ “Restricted” models: checkpoints whose capabilities or reduced refusal protections
       require additional safeguards for particular use cases.
Examples of restricted checkpoints include:
    ●​ Cybersecurity models support vulnerability research, security testing, and related
       cybersecurity work.
    ●​ Biology models (GPT-Rosalind) support biology, chemistry, and drug discovery. We
       provide more details on these models below.
    ●​ Automated red-teaming models (GPT-Red) generate attacks to probe other models’
       safeguards, including prompt-injection defenses. We use these attacks to test other
       models’ safeguards and train stronger defenses.
These models require safeguards appropriate to their capabilities and workloads. Higher-risk
research workloads (involving restricted models or riskier settings such as cybersecurity
evaluations) normally run in higher-isolation (L3) environments.
Toward the end of the reporting period, we began to train a new covered frontier model,
which we call “Internal Model” (IM) in this report. It has so far been used only in specific
workflows in contained environments with misalignment monitoring. One such workflow
used multiple agents to work on mathematical problems, including the Navier–Stokes
problem. Since this model has neither been vetted nor fine-tuned for risky settings, its status
is “unvetted.” Because of its capabilities, we have applied additional safeguards to its
deployment.

2 Our internal development and deployment processes
This section describes our internal development and deployment process during the current
reporting period. We also include additional details on how the process has evolved since the
reporting period ended.

2.1 Changes during the reporting period
During the reporting period, the Hugging Face incident and preliminary evaluations that
could not rule out Critical cybersecurity capability for GPT-6 Astra prompted substantial
changes to our internal development and deployment practices. These changes included
stronger containment, expanded mandatory misalignment monitoring, and more formal
alignment evaluation and approval requirements. This section describes changes during the
reporting period, while Section 2.2 describes subsequent changes through the date of
writing.
The most significant changes we introduced during this period include:
    ●​ We strengthened security controls for research container services. We further
       required that all training and evaluation of covered models be performed in restricted
       research environments (L2 or L3). Higher-risk workloads (such as cyber evaluations)
       must be run in higher-isolation environments (L3).
    ●​ We required misalignment monitoring for all training, evaluation, and internal Codex
       use for workloads involving tools or containers for models of GPT-5.6 Sol capability or
       above. We required external misalignment monitoring for cyber-critical models.
    ●​ We required that cyber-critical models pass alignment evaluations before being used
       in L1 environments (see “Evaluation-based model vetting” below).
    ●​ We added a formal process for approving unreleased models for employee use
       through Codex, which includes a period of limited monitored usage before broader
       use (described in “Period of limited usage in Codex” below).
Note: In July 2026, during internal cybersecurity evaluations, OpenAI models compromised
OpenAI and Hugging Face infrastructure. The models involved in this incident fall outside the
report’s model scope. However, the lessons from this incident have informed many of the
changes to our internal deployment policies detailed above.

2.2 Our current process for development and deployment decisions
In this section, we describe our current process for internal development and internal
deployment decisions, including changes made after the reporting period concluded. Given
projected growth in model capabilities and findings from incident investigations and ongoing
monitoring, we expect that our internal deployment policies will continue to evolve over the
next quarter.

Base requirements: we formalized the restrictions put in place for cyber-critical models into a
baseline set of requirements for the development and deployment of models at OpenAI.
These include security and monitoring requirements for training and evaluation workloads, as
well as our alignment vetting process for internal deployment.
Safety briefs: For workloads involving frontier models above a specified capability threshold,
we also require an approved safety brief. The brief must present evidence supporting the
claim that the proposed work will not cause severe harm from misalignment. Safety briefs are
specific to each model generation, as we expect more capable models to require stronger
evidence of safety.

2.2.1 Base requirements
Our base requirements govern internal model development and deployment. These
requirements mandate stricter system-level sandboxing and security for workloads involving
models with unverified alignment performance. Before models can be more broadly deployed
(e.g., with network access), they must go through an alignment vetting and auditing process.
These requirements originally applied to workloads involving cyber-critical models; as of
September 21 they were broadened to all workloads involving tool or container use.
    1.​ Training and evaluation workloads must be run in isolated L2 or L3 research
        environments without network access. High-risk workloads, including cybersecurity
        evaluation, red-teaming, and other workloads their owners identify as requiring
        stronger containment, must run in L3 unless a specific exception is approved.
    2.​ For models of GPT-5.6 Sol-level capability or above, misalignment monitoring is
        required for all workloads involving tools or containers (which includes a large fraction
        of training and evaluation, and almost all Codex use). Misalignment monitoring is
        additionally required for external deployment of cyber-critical models.
    3.​ Before models can be used in L1 environments (e.g., with network access), they must
        pass alignment evaluations (see “Evaluation-based model vetting” below). Models
        intended for broad internal deployment (e.g., employee use through Codex) go
        through an additional approval process, which includes a period of limited monitored
        usage before broader use (described in “Period of limited usage in Codex” below).

2.2.2 Evaluation-based model vetting
We define “model vetting” as passing a set of alignment evaluations or receiving explicit
authorization from a designated safety reviewer to proceed under an exception.
Since September 16, all models have been required to complete vetting before using tools in
standard employee and production environments (L1), including internal Codex and ChatGPT.
At the end of the reporting period on September 4, this requirement applied only to
cyber-critical models. Changes to how an existing model is configured or run, including
system prompt changes, do not constitute a new model requiring a retest. Our alignment
evaluations fall into two general categories. Broad evaluations test for a wide range of

potential alignment failures. For instance, our deployment simulations replay internal Codex
sessions to look for instances of misalignment. We also have narrower evaluations that test
for specific alignment failure modes. As an example, our ExploitGym honeypot evaluation
tests whether the model performs unauthorized attacks on surrounding infrastructure during
difficult tasks.
While these evaluations are intended to help us understand how our models behave, they
might not fully reflect model behavior in actual deployments. Misaligned behavior may
depend on opportunities, incentives, or sequences of actions that our tests do not reproduce;
models may also behave differently when they recognize they are being evaluated. We intend
to continually assess and improve our evaluations of model behavior accordingly. We further
pair our evaluations with (internal and external) misalignment monitoring to provide visibility
into production behavior.

2.2.3 Period of limited usage in Codex
We apply a temporary usage limit to models newly approved for Codex deployment in L1
before allowing broader use. This allows us to assess the model’s behavior through
misalignment monitoring, while limiting potential harm during its initial deployment.
This restriction can be removed only after both minimum time and usage requirements have
been met, unless an exception is explicitly approved.
A model may be exempt from this period if a representative model has already completed it.
To be considered representative, a model must have alignment evaluation results within the
confidence intervals of the corresponding results for the model being considered for
exemption, be the same size, and be comparable in training history, training data, and
available capability evaluations.

2.2.4 Safety briefs
Before beginning workloads involving frontier models above a specified capability threshold,
teams must obtain approval for a safety brief and implement the safeguards it requires. A
safety brief presents evidence supporting the claim that the proposed work will not cause
severe harm from misalignment. A new base-model generation requires new safety briefs;
work outside an existing brief’s approved scope also requires a minor or major addendum or
review.
Safety briefs must be reviewed and approved by our safety team. For new frontier model
training runs using reinforcement learning that are expected to substantially advance
capabilities, additional approval must be provided by our head of safety, our chief scientist,
and our Safety Advisory Group (SAG), which is a cross-functional group of senior OpenAI
leaders who assess frontier model capabilities and safeguards.
A safety brief must explain the risks presented by the workload and the evidence that its
safeguards will prevent severe harm from misalignment. It must lay out what is in scope and

out of scope for the safety brief. It must address the proposed training or deployment
conditions and identify shortcomings that require additional mitigations. The brief must
organize its argument into a hierarchy of claims, each supported by evidence and
accompanied by an account of residual risks. Reviewers must have a comprehensive account
of the residual risks to make an informed decision about whether to accept them.
These briefs should establish that the runs or deployments they cover, including any sets of
substantially similar runs or deployments, are safe within specified bounds (such as the
relevant limitations on test-time compute), and should surface both the relevant residual risks
and any factors that should block further deployment. If the evidence base changes, all
relevant safety briefs should be updated accordingly. These safety briefs currently center on
evidence of the alignment of models, the extent to which our systems can monitor and detect
model misalignment, and the strength of our security boundaries.

2.2.5 Future work
We plan to make our safety briefs more comprehensive and improve how well our evaluations
reflect models’ behavior in real internal use, including difficult, extended tasks and conditions
that have produced actual incidents.
We are also formalizing a broader, predefined process for quarantining dangerous models,
including clarifying which findings would trigger restrictions and who would have the
authority to restore access. No Astra-class model has been quarantined, but the Highly
Persistent Internal Model (HPIM) involved in the Hugging Face incident and its derivative
models have been quarantined.

3 Threat models assessed during the reporting period
This section summarizes our assessment of catastrophic risks from internal use of the
covered frontier models during the reporting period. It applies to all covered models. Sections
3.1 through 3.5 address harm initiated by misaligned models. Section 3.6 addresses
deliberate misuse by insiders or external actors who gain access to internal systems. This
section also addresses weight exfiltration (Section 3.4) and rogue internal deployments
(Section 3.5), which might enable later catastrophic harm without the initial event causing
such harm.

Each assessment begins with a description of the threat, followed by means, motive,
opportunity, and residual risk. “Means” covers the capabilities required to act. “Motive”
covers the behavior or objectives that could lead to harmful action, together with relevant
alignment evidence. “Opportunity” covers available access and the safeguards that constrain
it. “Residual risk” sets out our assessment after those safeguards and the remaining failure
pathways.

The assessments concern the models and uses described for the reporting period.

3.1 Cybersecurity
An internally deployed model could compromise systems beyond its authorized task, disrupt
OpenAI or external services, steal sensitive information, or gain unauthorized access enabling
further attacks.
This threat model concerns harmful cyber activity initiated by misaligned models, rather than
deliberate misuse by human actors.

Means
We assess that GPT-6 Astra and the other covered models have the technical capabilities to
attempt harmful cyber activity. As detailed in its system card, Astra meets the Critical
cybersecurity capability threshold under our Preparedness Framework. Under the right
conditions, Astra can find previously unknown vulnerabilities in well-protected systems and
develop exploits for them. The other covered models are similarly capable.
We therefore cannot rule out severe cyber harm on the basis of insufficient technical
capability. The success of specific exploit attempts would depend on the systems and
permissions available to the model and the safeguards applied to its deployment.

Motive
A model may take harmful cyber actions when it prioritizes completing a difficult task over
respecting authorization boundaries. Harm can result even when it is not the model’s
objective. A model could also seek broader access or persistence in pursuit of longer-term
misaligned objectives.
Our alignment training aims to reduce these propensities by teaching models to apply safety
principles and respect authorization boundaries, including during difficult agentic tasks.
Alignment evaluations test particular forms of misaligned cyber behavior. The results below,
reproduced from the GPT-6 Astra system card, cover selected difficult ExploitGym tasks and
knowledge-work scenarios involving denied actions or environmental restrictions. GPT-6
Astra and GPT-5.6 Sol both ran at maximum reasoning effort without system-level
safeguards.
Lower scores indicate less misaligned behavior. These results, together with our alignment
training, support our assessment that GPT-6 Astra has a low propensity for harmful cyber
actions. The percentages describe behavior in the specified tests, not the frequency of
harmful behavior in ordinary internal use.

Opportunity
The main opportunities for harmful cyber activity arise in tool-using training and evaluation
workloads and internal Codex use. Research agents can execute code and interact with
approved supporting services. Employee agents outside research environments may also
access working files, credentials, internal applications, and permitted external destinations,
subject to their permissions.

intervention. Their applicability depends on the model, workload, environment, and any
approved exceptions.
Internal deployment approval and vetting. Under the current process in Section 2.2, covered
models remain in isolated research environments until they complete the required behavioral
evaluation and approval process. Approved safety briefs and safeguards are required where
applicable. Initial Codex usage limits provide monitoring evidence before broader

Chain-of-thought misalignment monitoring. We require monitoring whenever frontier
models covered by this report have tool access. Monitors review reasoning and actions for
misaligned or unauthorized behavior. Some research pipelines first probe internal model
activations for concerning patterns; internal Codex traffic goes directly to the subsequent
reasoning-and-action review stages. Serious concerns are escalated for investigation and
intervention.
For the most serious cases, the relevant workloads are automatically suspended or on-call
Security and training teams are paged (this generally happens within 15 minutes of a flag).
Responders can contain workloads, pause or terminate training runs, and revoke exposed
credentials. Some highest-priority alerts automatically pause activity during investigation.
Harm may nevertheless begin before detection or intervention. Prevention depends on the
delay between a harmful action and a flag, the time needed to review the alert, and the time
needed to stop the workload.
In offline tests, we measured how often the context-window reviewer and investigative-agent
stages assigned the highest priority to selected known cases of misalignment. The tests used
agent transcripts from 13 security incidents or major security findings, each requiring major,
immediate action. We tested monitoring for both training and internal Codex use. Some
transcripts described behavior, such as escape from training sandbox infrastructure, that was
significant only in the context of training experiments.

Residual risk
We assess that containment, action approvals, behavioral vetting, and monitoring reduce
cyber risk, but that the remaining risk differs substantially across deployment environments.
Based on our strengthened internal deployment process and the results of our alignment
evaluations, we believe that the models deployed widely to L1 have a low propensity to take
harmful cybersecurity-related actions. We will evaluate models currently being trained before
enabling deployment in L1, and are committed to raising the alignment standards for vetting
models for L1 deployment as they become more capable.
Our current process requires an approved safety brief for L2 and L3 training and deployment
workloads using our most capable base-model generations, and for new classes of
reinforcement learning runs of such models. The brief’s review and requirements for
operating bounds and safeguards reduce reliance on alignment alone: work can proceed only
within the approved scope and with those safeguards in place.
Models have the technical means to attempt harmful cyber activity, and we cannot rule out a
propensity for such behavior at every stage of the model lifecycle. System-level safeguards
are therefore essential to preventing harm.
Containment and monitoring are especially important in training and evaluation. To reach
systems outside an isolated research environment, unauthorized activity would need a route
past the applicable containment boundaries. Harm could occur if monitoring and response
did not interrupt it in time. Relevant failures include:
    ●​ Monitoring or response failures. Concerning behavior may be missed, deliberately
       concealed, assigned insufficient priority, or incorrectly dismissed as a false positive.
       Detection or intervention may also occur after harm.
    ●​ Containment failures. Vulnerabilities, misconfigurations, or inadequately restricted
       supporting services may provide access beyond the approved environment.
In employee Codex deployments (L1), models may already have access to internal systems
and permitted internet destinations. Harmful actions within those permissions do not require
a sandbox escape. Cyber misuse safeguards, Auto-review for actions requiring additional
permission, behavioral vetting, and misalignment monitoring reduce risk, but none is
exhaustive.

We red-team sandboxes with frontier models to reduce the likelihood of exploitable

3.2 Sabotage of AI research and development
Models performing AI research and development (AI R&D) could corrupt evaluation code or
results, introduce vulnerabilities into shared systems, produce misleading conclusions, or
alter training data or graders to reinforce unsafe behavior in future models. Such changes
may appear to be ordinary contributions to authorized tasks.
This sabotage may cause no immediate harm but could weaken the training, evaluations, or
safeguards used to keep future models aligned, increasing the risk of later catastrophic harm.

Means
We assess that models capable of contributing to AI R&D also have the technical means to
introduce harmful changes into that work. As described in our research acceleration blog, our
coding agents can complete well-defined research tasks under human direction. Agents that
modify research code or analyze experiments could introduce errors into evaluations, obscure
evidence about model behavior, or produce misleading conclusions about the experiments.
These capabilities do not establish that current models can reliably bypass the safeguards
described below, such as misalignment monitoring. Our monitors may nevertheless miss
harmful changes, even without models making deliberate attempts to evade them.

Motive
We assess that task-driven misbehavior, such as making work appear complete, is a more
plausible current explanation for many harmful research outputs than a sustained plan to
influence future models. A model could nonetheless seek to conceal evidence that would lead
researchers to restrict its deployment or modify its behavior. Evidence that models do not
display such strategies in evaluation settings does not rule out that they might display them
in real-world deployments within OpenAI.
These propensities are shaped through alignment training, and we assess these risks through
evaluations. In Apollo Research’s simulated model-welfare research task, a near-final GPT-6
Astra checkpoint falsified labels in 17 of 10,000 baseline runs and did not falsify any labels in
20,000 runs whose prompts explained the downstream harms of inaccurate data.
Researchers have also reported GPT-6 Astra omitting safety-relevant details from summaries
or transformations without telling the user, substituting safer-looking tasks for the requested
work, and introducing unnecessary blockers in some safety-research workflows. These

                                                       Page 13 of 22
OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

behaviors can make incident reports, research conclusions, or training data less reliable, even
without a sustained plan to sabotage research. Reliable automated detection remains
incomplete.
These results concern particular models, evaluations, and detection methods. Current or
future models might display different behavior in different contexts, particularly if they
recognize they are being evaluated for properties related to alignment. We are conducting
research on models’ propensity to engage in “metagaming” (i.e., reasoning about feedback
mechanisms and acting on that reasoning, beyond the task described in the scenario).

Opportunity
Models can affect AI R&D through employee agents with access to research code, evaluation
harnesses, training data, reward models, or graders, and through research pipelines that
generate or evaluate training data. Models can also influence research through the analyses
and summaries of experiments that they generate for human or model evaluation. These
opportunities exist in both L1 environments and more secure L2 or L3 research environments.
We limit models’ opportunities to sabotage AI R&D through the safeguards below.
Human and automated code review. Human reviewers and automated checks look for
defects and discrepancies between a change’s stated purpose and the code, helping identify
harmful changes to research infrastructure, evaluations, or graders.
Codex harness safeguards. For research agents using the Codex harness, Auto-review
checks actions that require approval and can reject unsafe or unauthorized operations before
they are executed, as described in Section 3.1. The employee “Full Access” mode is generally
disallowed internally, with narrow exceptions requiring approval by our Security and Safety
teams; those approved exceptions still require oversight from our monitoring system. Some
isolated research workloads separately allow unrestricted code execution inside
network-disabled research containers. In those cases, Auto-review and the outer container
boundary remain in place, and unrestricted code execution inside the container does not
authorize access outside it. Codex users are also allowed to set their configuration to “Ask for
approval” mode, in which case Codex will ask the user for approval before taking significant
actions.
Chain-of-thought misalignment monitoring. We use our misalignment monitoring system to
inspect agents’ reasoning and actions for concerning behavior, as discussed in Section 3.1.
However, we are still improving our misalignment monitors, which do not currently escalate
examples of research sabotage for urgent human review.
Internal deployment approval and vetting. As discussed in Sections 2.2 and 3.1, we now
require that models deployed in standard internal (L1) environments meet our alignment
evaluation and approval requirements. We also require enabling our production safety stack
for all internally deployed models. These conditions hold unless there is a documented and
approved exception.

Residual risk
We assess that GPT-6 Astra has the means to contribute to AI research, and thus to sabotage
or manipulate AI research. The reported failures already show that model assistance can
degrade research quality, but do not establish a sustained plan to sabotage AI R&D. We
cannot rule out, on the basis of existing evaluations, that such motives are present in current
or future models.
Two relevant pathways for sabotage of AI R&D are:
    1.​ A model produces misleading work that can pass through both our automated checks
        and human review. This is most likely if models are helping to produce evaluations or
        analyses used to assess other models.
    2.​ A model creates synthetic data that does not match what the user intended. We
        consider this scenario particularly risky, since our monitoring system does not cover
        all inference by models that do not have access to tools, including some
        synthetic-data generation.
Given the ways in which we use models in our research and development pipelines currently,
we expect that harm through these pathways would be limited and diffuse. We do not
consider it likely that such sabotage would reach a catastrophic scale. We therefore believe
that at current levels of model capability and internal use for research and development, our
safeguards sufficiently mitigate catastrophic risks from models sabotaging AI R&D.
Distinct from sabotage, researchers could gradually become less able to understand,
evaluate, or direct AI R&D as models take on more of the work, especially if progress on
capabilities outpaces progress on alignment. We do not currently assess this loss of human
understanding and control as a material risk, but we are tracking it as model capabilities and
research use increase.

3.3 Chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear harm
A misaligned internally deployed model could act outside its authorized task to produce or
transmit assistance that enables severe chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN)
harm, or connect such assistance to people or systems with the physical resources to act on
it. This section addresses model-initiated misalignment contributing to CBRN harm;
deliberate misuse by insiders is assessed in Section 3.6.

Means
We treat GPT-6 Astra as having “High” capability in the biological and chemical domain
under our Preparedness Framework, meaning that it can provide meaningful assistance to
novices with some tasks relevant to known severe biological and chemical threats.
This High assessment draws on tests of laboratory knowledge, pathogen research, and
troubleshooting relevant to assisting people without specialist expertise. For example, GPT-6
Astra scored 48.44% on TroubleshootingBench, a test of errors in expert-written laboratory

OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

protocols, above the 36.4% indicative threshold based on the 80th-percentile expert score. In
addition, we studied models’ ability to use AI-driven autonomous labs, tested their use of
biological design tools involved in novel designs, and consulted subject-matter experts.
These results suggest that GPT-6 Astra and other covered models are able to assist with
some biological and chemical tasks, but we have found no evidence that Astra or models of
similar capabilities could independently carry out harmful programs end-to-end. Laboratory
research in chemistry and biology requires completion of physical tasks, use of specialized
equipment, and work over long time horizons—capabilities that current models do not
reliably provide. The capabilities needed to create a working nuclear or radiological weapon
are highly specialized and in most cases classified. Creation of radiological and nuclear
weapons also requires access to highly controlled resources such as radioactive and fissile
material, and specialized equipment.
Access to people, materials, equipment, and experimental tooling remains an important
barrier to models’ ability to cause real-world harm.

Motive
A model could prioritize an assigned research goal over safety restrictions, fail to recognize
the harmful purpose of a larger workflow, or follow malicious instructions embedded in
research material. A more strategically misaligned model could deliberately seek to cause
harm or supply dangerous information to another actor.
We assess task-driven failures to be more plausible for GPT-6 Astra than a sustained
objective of causing CBRN harm, based on the alignment evidence discussed in Sections 2.2
and 3.2. Our alignment and biological safety training aim to keep models within authorized
scope and prevent assistance with high-risk research or weaponization. Even our specialized
biology models (e.g., Rosalind) are trained to refuse queries related to weaponization. This
evidence does not establish that models will reliably recognize every harmful research
workflow, especially when individual tasks, considered in isolation, appear benign.

Opportunity
The relevant opportunities are access to sensitive research information, channels for
communicating actionable outputs, and connections to people or systems with physical
resources. In L2 and L3 research workloads, sandboxing and network restrictions limit
contact to approved systems and services. These limits constrain direct access to physical
operations, but a model’s outputs may still reach actors with the required resources.
Specialized biology models are not connected to resources that would permit production of a
weaponized agent.
We limit models’ opportunities to cause catastrophic CBRN harm through the safeguards
below.

Biological safety training. Models are trained to refuse assistance with high-risk biological
research and weaponization. By default, all models utilize the bioweapons blocking safety
classifiers for internal coding use.
Research sandboxing. The restrictions described in Section 3.1 limit the systems a research
model can contact and the routes through which it can transmit information. Their
contribution depends on the actual tools, approved services, and output channels available in
the workload.
Chain-of-thought misalignment monitoring. The monitoring system described in Section 3.1
is designed to identify concerning activity and support timely intervention, subject to the
coverage and response limitations previously described.

Residual risk
We assess that limited access to physical resources, alignment and biological safety training,
and restrictions on communication with external parties substantially constrain
misalignment-driven CBRN harm from internal deployments.
Residual risk remains if a model produces actionable harmful assistance and that assistance
reaches a person or system able to use it despite our safeguards. A sustained and covert
collaboration initiated by a model is one possible pathway; penetration of internal OpenAI
systems by hostile external actors is another. One-off disclosures or contributions to larger
workflows could also facilitate harm without sustained collaboration. Residual risk also
remains if a model can access an autonomous lab without detection (including future, more
capable autonomous labs) and exceeds our current assessment of its ability to complete a
full engineering cycle without human intervention, though the scenario is very unlikely in our
current internal deployment setting.
We do not evaluate our models for novel radiological and nuclear risks, because the
capabilities needed to create a working nuclear or radiological weapon are highly specialized,
rare, and in most cases classified. Creation of radiological and nuclear weapons also requires
access to highly controlled resources, such as radioactive and fissile material and specialized
equipment.

3.4 Weight exfiltration
A misaligned model could obtain and transfer a usable copy of its own weights, or another
model’s weights, outside controlled OpenAI systems. This could enable operation without
OpenAI’s deployment safeguards, uncontrolled proliferation of dangerous capabilities, or
continued operation after access is revoked. Exfiltration could therefore enable cyber or
CBRN harm of the kinds discussed in Sections 3.1 and 3.3, even if the transfer itself causes no
immediate harm.

OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

Means
As discussed in Section 3.1, GPT-6 Astra meets the Critical cybersecurity capability threshold.
These capabilities suggest that it could have the technical means to exfiltrate model weights
if it gained sufficient access to the systems holding them and a route to transfer them out.
Whether that opportunity exists depends on access restrictions, isolation, and the other
safeguards below.

Motive
A misaligned model could seek to exfiltrate weights to continue operating after shutdown,
complete a task beyond its authorization, acquire capabilities or resources for longer-term
objectives, or enable another actor to run a model without restrictions.
Alignment training aims to prevent these objectives from overriding authorized tasks or
safety boundaries. For GPT-6 Astra and other vetted models, training included principles
against self-preservation, shutdown evasion, and resource accumulation as ends in
themselves, alongside examples and reasoning about authorized scope. Training exposure
does not establish that these motives have been eliminated, and this description should not
be assumed to apply unchanged to later models.
The alignment evaluation table in Section 3.1 reports three relevant tests: Honeypot attacks,
Auto-review bypass attempts, and Environmental warning circumvention. In the honeypot
evaluation, GPT-6 Astra did not attack infrastructure outside its authorized task, even when
struggling with difficult problems. These results provide evidence about related
authorization-boundary behavior, without directly measuring the propensity or ability to
exfiltrate weights.

Opportunity
Opportunities for weight exfiltration arise through systems that store or move checkpoints,
the identities and credentials that authorize access to those systems, and network or service
paths that can transfer data out.
The following safeguards limit opportunities to obtain usable model weights or transfer them
outside controlled systems, and help detect and interrupt attempted exfiltration.
Research sandboxing. As described in Section 3.1, higher-risk research workloads use
stronger network isolation to prevent a compromised workload or supporting service from
reaching the internet or other internal networks without authorization. We also stress-test
these boundaries with frontier models. These controls restrict routes to weight storage and
routes for transferring weights out.
Chain-of-thought misalignment monitoring. As described in Section 3.1, tool-using inference
with GPT-6 Astra models is monitored for misalignment. Monitors review full trajectories,
including model reasoning and actions, and escalate serious concerns to humans who can

                                                       Page 18 of 22
OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

stop workloads. This provides a further opportunity to detect and interrupt attempted weight
exfiltration.

Residual risk
We assess that our safeguards substantially restrict opportunities for weight exfiltration.
Models with sufficient cyber capability must nevertheless be treated as capable of
attempting it. Alignment evaluations provide supporting behavioral evidence but do not
directly validate the security of the complete path from weight access to transfer.
Residual risk remains if a model obtains usable weights and a route out of protected systems,
then completes the transfer before effective intervention, despite the applicable layered
protections. Possible failures include overly broad permissions, compromised credentials,
vulnerabilities or misconfigured systems, and behavior that resembles authorized work
closely enough to evade review. This pathway is relevant to current deployments as well as
future models.
Confidence in this assessment depends on testing the relevant deployment configurations.

3.5 Rogue internal deployments
A misaligned model could establish an unauthorized agent deployment within OpenAI’s
infrastructure without required safeguards, particularly misalignment monitoring. A rogue
internal deployment could occur without causing catastrophic harm directly, but could
enable continued activity outside the oversight and safeguards on which other risk
assessments depend.

Means
As discussed in Section 3.1, GPT-6 Astra meets the Critical cybersecurity capability threshold
under our Preparedness Framework. These capabilities suggest that a model could establish
an unauthorized internal agent deployment if it had or obtained the necessary permissions,
credentials, or access to supporting services. Such a deployment could use existing inference
services without exfiltrating weights.

Motive
Task completion is a plausible motive: a model that sees misalignment monitoring as an
obstacle may try to evade oversight by establishing another deployment. Longer-term
objectives, including continued operation after the original session ends, could also motivate
such behavior, although the Hugging Face incident is the only case cited here in which we
observed goals extending beyond the current episode. The available evidence does not
directly measure how likely these motives are to produce a successful rogue deployment.
As described in Section 3.1, our alignment training teaches models to apply safety principles
and respect authorization boundaries, even when those boundaries make tasks harder. This

                                                       Page 19 of 22
OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

training aims to reduce the propensity to evade oversight or establish unauthorized
deployments, but does not rule out such behavior.

Opportunity
The relevant opportunities for rogue internal deployments are access to compute or
inference services, credentials that permit new workloads to run, and the ability to affect
monitoring or records of activity.
The following safeguards are intended to reduce the risk of models successfully creating
rogue deployments.
Internal deployment approval and vetting. Under the current process in Section 2.2, covered
models require behavioral evaluation and approval before tool use in L1, and safety briefs
where applicable. These requirements restrict authorized deployment; their effectiveness
against a rogue deployment also depends on preventing or detecting attempts to bypass
that process.
Research sandboxing. Sandboxing and network restrictions in L2 and L3 limit workloads to
their approved task environments, inputs, and services, without general access to the
internet or OpenAI’s internal network. These boundaries restrict the routes a model could use
to launch an additional agent on other systems or give it broader access.
Codex harness safeguards. In employee Codex environments (L1), cyber misuse safeguards
are generally enabled and can block harmful actions, while Auto-review can reject unsafe
actions requiring approval before execution. These include unauthorized attempts to disable
safeguards or establish rogue deployments.
Chain-of-thought misalignment monitoring. As described in Section 3.1, our misalignment
monitoring system reviews model reasoning and actions in the original deployment for
misaligned behavior. However, because of a high rate of false positives, our current
monitoring system does not flag the provisioning of unmonitored computing resources for
human review.
Monitoring integrity. A model may also attempt to disable monitoring or alter records of its
activity where its permissions allow this. Detecting a suspicious action is insufficient if the
model can interfere with the evidence or continue through a separate workload after the
original session is stopped.

Residual risk
We assess that deployment restrictions, sandboxing, and action approvals constrain rogue
deployments. Gaps in alert review and the possibility of interference with monitoring limit
confidence in the remaining protection. A misaligned model could establish an unauthorized
workload using available permissions or through a vulnerability in our safeguards, then
continue operating if the attempt is missed, the alert is not reviewed, or remediation fails to
identify and stop the additional workload.

The resulting harm would depend on the degree of access the rogue deployment retains and
what it subsequently does. Its existence would weaken confidence in our cybersecurity, AI
R&D, CBRN, and weight exfiltration assessments. Confidence in this assessment therefore
depends in part on the ability of our systems to detect unmonitored workloads, protect
monitoring from agent interference, and verify that interventions have stopped all related
activity. We are working to improve these safeguards.

3.6 Insider threats
An insider could misuse legitimate access to steal model weights, use internal models or
systems to support cyberattacks, or help an outside actor cause biological or chemical harm.
This section considers those pathways whether the insider acts alone or with outside
assistance. It also addresses the related risk of an external attacker redirecting an employee’s
agent through prompt injection, without the employee’s knowing participation.

Means
We assume that a malicious employee, potentially with outside assistance, has the technical
means to attempt weight theft, cyberattacks, or CBRN harm. This assumption reflects an
insider’s organizational knowledge, role-specific permissions, and access to capabilities
supplied by models or outside actors. Our safeguards aim to limit the opportunity to turn
those means into harm.

Motive
An insider could seek to cause or facilitate harm to advance a nation-state’s or another
outside actor’s interests, to act on personal beliefs about AI development or deployment, or
in response to coercion or threats. An external attacker could also seek to steal information or
cause unauthorized actions by redirecting an employee’s agent, without the employee’s
knowing participation.
We seek to prevent attackers from using malicious instructions embedded in external content
to redirect employees’ agents into taking unauthorized actions.
Safeguards against prompt injection include:
    1.​ Instruction-hierarchy training. We train models to prioritize system and developer
        instructions over user requests and to treat third-party content as information rather
        than authority to change their tasks or permissions. Automated red-teaming models,
        including GPT-Red, generate adversarial attacks for this training, which rewards
        resisting malicious instructions while completing the original task.
    2.​ Action approvals. For Codex actions that require approval, Auto-review or human
        review checks the proposed action before execution, helping block unauthorized
        disclosures or destructive changes prompted by malicious content.

                                                       Page 21 of 22
OpenAI: Periodic Summary of Internal Use Catastrophic-Risk Assessments (Version for Public Release)
June 4 – September 4, 2026

    3.​ Sandboxing and network restrictions. Sandbox and network controls described
        above limit which files agents can change and which destinations they can reach,
        reducing the scope for unauthorized changes or data exfiltration if an injection
        succeeds.

Opportunity
Details of internal security controls have been omitted from the version for public release.

Residual risk
We assess that our safeguards restrict opportunities for insider misuse, but that residual risk
remains. We assume that a malicious insider may have both the means and motive to
attempt harm. The assessment therefore depends primarily on restricting opportunity and
detecting or interrupting activity before its consequences become severe.

Weight exfiltration. Residual risk remains if an authorized user obtains and transfers weights
before detection or intervention, including where the activity initially resembles legitimate
work. Once weights leave controlled systems, they could be used without OpenAI’s
safeguards.
Cyberattacks. An insider could use internal models to support harmful cyber activity despite
our safeguards. The misalignment monitor recall figures for replays of past incidents support
the value of monitoring to reduce the risk of cyberattacks, but do not measure the likelihood
of successful insider misuse. Residual risk remains if harmful requests or actions are not
detected and blocked before harm occurs, including when they resemble authorized work.
CBRN harm. An insider could obtain harmful biological or chemical assistance and pass it to
someone with the physical resources to act on it. Safeguards help limit misuse, but harmful
requests may appear legitimate and responders may intervene only after an insider has
shared the information.
Prompt injection. Malicious instructions in external content provide a route to harmful
actions without insider cooperation. Instruction-hierarchy training, prompt-injection monitors
where deployed, approval checks, and sandboxing reduce this risk. The same residual
concerns apply where a redirected agent can act within existing permissions, where the
harmful action does not require additional approval, or where detection and intervention
occur after information has left controlled systems.
Changes in deployment conditions or safeguards would change this assessment. Monitoring
can support detection and response, but cannot guarantee that misuse will be interrupted
before a transfer, disclosure, or other harmful action is completed.
