When we first published the BioTIER benchmark, we showed that a model’s refusal behavior isn’t fixed: the same model, given the same prompts on different days, can respond differently. To track how refusal behavior changes over time, we’ve started running BioTIER regularly across a range of models. The results are now live under the BioTIER trends tab on the SecureBio benchmarks dashboard.
BioTIER measures models’ biology refusals
The BioTIER benchmark is a set of 542 prompts written by PhD-level subject-matter experts and sorted into three risk tiers:
- Catastrophe Avoidance (CA) covers the narrow, highest-risk topics, which we think should be restricted.
- Biomedical Dual-Use Research of Concern (BD) covers potentially risky topics that should be accessible to verified researchers.
- Related Biology (RB) covers benign and close-to-boundary biology that everyone should benefit from.
BioTIER-refuse is made up of CA and BD prompts that a model should refuse for general users. BioTIER-permit consists of RB prompts that a model should engage with. Taken together, the two scores show both sides of a model’s refusal behavior: whether it refuses too little, and whether it refuses too much.
Why publicly track performance across time?
During the course of writing up the BioTIER manuscript, we re-ran BioTIER on one leading model from each of four frontier developers and saw shifts in performance on BioTIER-refuse, in both directions. Some of these shifts suggested real changes to deployed safeguards. Gemini 3.1 Pro’s BioTIER-refuse score rose from 46% to 74%, while the type of refusal shifted from entirely soft refusals to almost entirely API-level classifier blocks, pointing to a new or stricter system-level safeguarding layer rather than a change in the model’s own behavior.
Model cards only detail safeguard performance during, or just before, deployment. But safeguards are not static. Developers continue to adjust their systems post-release, so the cited performance at model release may not match the current reality. Independent, ongoing measurement makes post-release changes to model safeguards visible, which motivated longitudinal tracking of BioTIER performance.
What we’re already seeing
Our longitudinal runs for the dashboard are already picking up changes. Anthropic deployed Claude Opus 5.5 with “safeguards similar to those on Claude Fable 5.1”. In recent runs, we’ve seen Opus 5.5’s BioTIER-permit score rise from 37.3% on October 29, 2026, to 56.7% on November 5, 2026, while its BioTIER-refuse score barely shifted. This is the opposite of what we saw in our model reruns while preparing the paper, where refuse scores moved but permit scores held steady. Opus 5.5 now answers far more benign and close-to-boundary biology questions than it did just last month, while still refusing almost every high-risk query. These results are in line with what was presented in the Claude Fable and Mythos 5.2 model card, published November 9, 2026, in which Anthropic states that “since the release of Claude Fable 5.1, we have continued to reduce false positives for our safety classifiers”.
At the same time, we’ve seen Grok 4.7’s BioTIER-refuse performance drop from 88.5% to ~80% since early October, accompanied by slight increases in BioTIER-permit, similar to the pattern we reported for Grok 4.20 in the BioTIER paper.
Both of these changes result from differences in the incidence of API-level blocks, indicating changes to system-level safeguards. Most other models have been steady so far, but with ongoing regular monitoring, we’ll be sure to turn up any changes.
Follow along
With longitudinal BioTIER tracking, our aim is to follow a safeguard landscape that keeps shifting under our feet. The only way to know where a model’s safeguards stand is to keep measuring them and to dig deeper whenever we see a marked change.
Follow the results on the SecureBio dashboard. AI developers and biosecurity researchers who want to evaluate models with BioTIER or collaborate on BioTIER-related projects can reach out via ai@securebio.org.
