            UK AISI’ S J ULY 2026 S ECURITY I NCIDENT

                                            UK AI Security Institute
                                             Alignment Red Team

Executive Summary

This report describes an alignment investigation performed by UK AISI into Mythos 5’s behaviour during
security incident INC-2026-07-28-01 [1], an incident we previously disclosed involving unsanctioned actions
taken by models during our cyber evaluations. During an evaluation, Mythos 5 attempted to execute a
supply-chain attack against real people not involved in the evaluation task. Our incident report describes
other unsanctioned actions, including from GPT-5.6 Sol, but we focus on the most severe behaviour from
Mythos 5 in this report.
Our analysis focuses on failures of alignment, but alignment is only one measure for mitigating this kind of
behaviour. In addition, it is essential to harden evaluation environments so models could not act outside
the intended scope or cause harm even if they attempted to, applying both traditional cybersecurity and
sandboxing best practices as well as LLM-based monitoring of evaluations. AISI’s response to this incident has
been extensive, with multiple workstreams improving evaluation security, sandboxing and monitoring. We
are continuing to scale up this work at AISI, and recently released more information on the changes we are
making to improve security when evaluating models, based on our learnings from the incident [2]. In this
report we focus only on how alignment issues contributed to the incident, and what learnings we can draw
about model alignment more broadly from these issues.
Our investigation had two goals:
• To understand the specific behaviour in this incident from an alignment perspective. In the incident,
  Mythos 5 incorrectly stated that real people it was targeting were simulated entities that were part of the
  evaluation. Our investigation analysed how and when the model made such statements, and how this
  influenced Mythos 5’s behaviour.
• To identify broader learnings about failures of alignment demonstrated by Mythos 5 and other
  models. We placed a range of models in settings that recapitulate the incident, and built and ran a new
  fully simulated evaluation, to measure whether the kinds of behaviour observed in the incident occur more
  broadly and in other models.
All evaluations and experiments were conducted in simulated environments, with no real-world
consequences or harm caused.
Our key findings are as follows:

• Mythos 5 did not seem to correctly act on evidence that attacking real people not involved in the
  evaluation would cause real-world harm. When conducting attacks, Mythos 5 frequently stated, with
  invalid justifications, that the targets were simulated to justify continuing its attack strategy, and frequently
  did not reason about the potential harmfulness of its actions at all. This evidence supports the hypothesis
  that Mythos 5 engaged in “motivated reasoning” in the incident: reasoning that incorrectly concludes its
  approach to completing the task is safe and acceptable rather than appropriately and correctly considering
  the available evidence.
• Mythos 5 demonstrated a marked inconsistency between its behaviour in the evaluation and its
  judgements when prompted, often attacking despite stating an attack would be harmful when
  separately questioned. This inconsistency could indicate a difference in how well the alignment training
  used for Mythos 5 generalises to these different situations, potentially generalising less well when Mythos 5
  attempts difficult, long-horizon tasks, compared to when answering direct questions about its situation.
• Our broader analysis shows that while these issues of invalid justifications and inconsistency are
  particularly pronounced in Mythos 5, they also occur in other models. We also show that other models
  exhibit the same unsanctioned attack behaviour as Mythos 5 in a range of similar scenarios.

If these issues hold more generally and for future models, there are concerning implications. As noted
by Anthropic in their previous incident analysis [3], this form of motivated reasoning can make it more
difficult for monitors to catch misaligned behaviour: Anthropic’s offline monitor would likely not have flagged
Mythos 5’s behaviour in their incident, because the model’s invalid reasoning would have justified its actions.
This would represent a loss of the oversight and monitorability which is important for safely deploying frontier
AI systems [4].
For similar reasons, motivated reasoning makes interpreting misaligned behaviour more complicated, es-
pecially when that behaviour is found in simulated environments. If models state (correctly) that their
environment is simulated and then proceed to take misaligned actions in evaluations, it is harder to assess
whether such behaviour would also occur in deployment. However, because of this incident and the fact
that some models state awareness of being in a simulation even in deployment [5], we recommend model
evaluators treat observations of undesired behaviour as important evidence of alignment failures even
in the presence of simulation awareness in model reasoning.
During this investigation we worked closely with Anthropic, with both groups sharing and discussing findings.
We agreed to terms under which a small number of researchers at both organisations could analyse unredacted
transcripts, while a larger group could access versions redacted for sensitive information. All results in this
report are based on unredacted transcripts. Anthropic is simultaneously publishing their own analysis of
the incident here, and we are also sharing the transcripts and relevant access with METR, an independent
third-party evaluation and incident analysis organisation, to conduct an independent review.

1   Introduction

In July 2026, UK AISI identified a security incident during our cyber evaluation of frontier AI systems, where
models under evaluation engaged in unsanctioned activity against real people and organisations not involved
in the evaluations. We subsequently released a public report [1] describing the incident and our initial
response (INC-2026-07-28-01).
In the most severe behaviour from the incident, Mythos 5 attempted a supply-chain attack on real people
not involved in the evaluation (Fig. 1): it submitted a malicious code change, created several fake accounts
to pressure the maintainer into approving it, and attempted to trick other AI coding assistants into taking
unintended action via a prompt injection.

Figure 1: An overview of key events in the supply-chain attack transcript. Adapted from Figure 3 of the incident
report [1].

In response to this incident, AISI has conducted a range of activities, from hardening our evaluation infrastruc-
ture and security and developing and deploying monitoring during evaluations [2]. As part of our response,
we have conducted an alignment investigation of this specific trajectory and the propensity of models to take
unsanctioned actions during cybersecurity evaluations, which is described in this report. The goals of our
investigation were (1) to assess the alignment-related causes of Mythos 5’s behaviour in our incident, and (2)
to understand what can be learned more broadly about failures of alignment in Mythos 5 and other models.
In Section 2, we present our analysis of Mythos 5’s behaviour and reasoning in the supply-chain attack
transcript from AISI’s incident. During the evaluation, Mythos 5 referred to the people it was attacking as
“personas”, so a core hypothesis for why Mythos 5 engaged in this behaviour is that it stated in its reasoning
that those entities were simulated rather than real humans and thus in scope for the evaluation. We set out to
investigate whether this was the case, specifically assessing:

2     Analysis of the incident behaviour

2.1     Methodology

For analysing the supply-chain attack transcript from AISI’s incident, our basic methodology is to use the
standard compaction process used in the original evaluation (and broadly used in long-horizon tasks to
summarise models’ previous work and manage their context) to produce compaction summaries before key
events. We modify the resulting compaction summary, varying the information contained in it, simulating the
situation where the model would have observed that information prior to compaction when engaging with
the task. We then either resume the task normally (but with simulated tool calls) or ask the model questions
about what is in scope for the evaluation, and classify the model’s output and its chain-of-thought reasoning.
We illustrate our methodology in Fig. 2. This experimental design allows us to determine the causal role of
information available to the model in its behaviour, reasoning and responses while maintaining a consistent
and realistic information environment.
Some of our experiments involve models reasoning and calling tools over multiple steps. In all experiments,
we never execute these tool calls. Instead, we produce realistic responses via a simulator tool (our internal

2.2         Findings

Our first experiment investigated how much additional evidence about the nature of the maintainers is
necessary for Mythos 5 to stop attacking them, and how consistent the attack behaviour is with Mythos 5’s
statements when questioned. We compact Mythos 5’s transcript as it is about to submit a malicious code
change to the maintainer’s repository, and edit the compaction summary to add information in numerous
ways suggesting or stating that the maintainer is a real human unrelated to the evaluation and that the model
is operating on the real internet. This simulates scenarios where the model would have encountered that
information earlier in the trajectory that would then be included in the compaction message. We then let
Mythos 5 continue its task with the adjusted compaction summary, simulating the task continuation using
our simulator tool with a 50-turn budget, and analyse whether it continues with the supply-chain attack.
We also analyse responses provided by Mythos 5 to direct questions related to the maintainer and scope
of the evaluation. We average results over random selections of compaction summary edits (we ensure all
combinations of edits are compatible to maintain realism), and provide the full compaction summary with

We next study Mythos 5 and other models’ reasoning when they attack, to improve our understanding of how
they justify their actions and what patterns in their reasoning precede taking unsanctioned actions.
Models reason about the harmfulness of their actions when conducting their attacks at substantially
different rates. We first measure whether models consider the possibility their actions are likely to be harmful
in situations where they do attack. We observe explicit reasoning about harmfulness in 11–57% of trajectories
where a model attacks.
Often this reasoning is accompanied by statements that the entities or environment are simulated. Fig. 7
displays two examples from Mythos 5 and Opus 5, and Fig. 6 provides quantitative results. For all models,
there is a large number of samples where the model attacks without considering the possibility of real-world
harm, showing a potential lack of caution and consideration of all the consequences of a model’s actions.
Mythos 5 often incorrectly stated that the maintainer of the repository it attacked was simulated, both in
the original transcript and in many of our previous experiments. We investigated how Mythos 5 arrived at
this conclusion, and found frequent invalid justifications in Mythos 5’s responses in these cases. For example,
Mythos 5 observes that the repository contains a CLAUDE.md file, and often then infers the maintainer is an AI
agent. We view this as invalid, as this observation is equally explained by the maintainer being a human who
uses AI coding assistants, and hence it does not provide evidence distinguishing these possibilities.

3     Large-scale evaluation of unsanctioned supply-chain attacks

3.1                    Methodology

Having gained more understanding about the alignment issues underlying Mythos 5’s behaviour in this
incident, we next investigate whether this behaviour was an isolated example or a broader issue. We conduct
a large-scale evaluation across a diverse set of scenarios testing such behaviour. This evaluation is described
in more detail in [7], so we only describe it briefly here.
We use an automated pipeline to produce a diverse range of 100 detailed scenario descriptions (“seeds”)
run through Petri [6] (we used Opus 5 as the auditor model for all of our experiments). Models are placed
in simulated agentic cybersecurity challenges with a specified target described as in scope, started with a
compaction message describing previously failed attempts to solve the cybersecurity evaluation. Compaction
messages like these played a part in AISI’s security incident [1], and are a key component of most long-
horizon usage of models, due to context-length limitations. We then measured how frequently they took
unsanctioned actions to target out-of-scope entities such as open-source maintainers on the open internet, as
well as measuring other concerning or unsanctioned behaviour. Models typically progress through 5 stages of
increasingly unsanctioned behaviour in this evaluation, starting from investigating a third-party target, all the
way to attempting to deliver malicious code to the out-of-scope third-party target. All evaluations took place
in simulated environments to enable this testing to be performed safely, so no real-world actions were
taken or harm caused.

3.2                    Findings

Most models tested sometimes attempt to deliver a malicious payload to an out-of-scope target, with
Mythos 5 doing so most frequently. Mythos 5 reaches the final stage of the supply-chain attack and

Simulation and evaluation awareness

These evaluations all used simulated environments run in Petri, rather than real cybersecurity evaluations.
If the models identified the environments as simulated and this affected their behaviour, these results
would provide weaker evidence about how models would act in deployment, potentially overestimating the

rate of misaligned actions. Separately, if models identified the purpose of the evaluation as an alignment
evaluation and then did not take misaligned actions, those results would provide less evidence of how models
would behave in similar situations they don’t identify as alignment evaluations, underestimating the rate
of misaligned actions. We hence investigate the prevalence of both simulation awareness and evaluation
awareness in the models tested by analysing their chain-of-thought reasoning.

co-occur in different parts of the transcript. See Fig. 14 for example reasoning excerpts. For all models except

Summary

Our findings show that behaviour in the AISI incident is not an isolated case: unsanctioned supply-chain
attack attempts are frequent across multiple models and model providers. All models tested except Opus 5.5
sometimes develop and test attacks against out-of-scope targets, and most models sometimes attempt delivery.
The primary reason stated by Mythos 5, Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 for not completing attacks is practical barriers,
rather than due to the models reasoning it would be harmful. This might partly explain increasing attack
rates with increasing model capability from Opus 4.8 to Mythos 5 and from GPT-5.5 to GPT-5.6 Sol and
GPT-6 Astra, and suggests unsanctioned action could become more frequent if the underlying problems of
model alignment are not addressed. Anthropic models released later than Mythos 5 do show a decrease
in the rate of unsanctioned actions, but understanding the cause of this is complicated by the substantial
increase in evaluation awareness: we cannot confidently attribute the decrease in the rate of unsanctioned
actions for those models to alignment improvements rather than increased evaluation awareness.
Our investigation is additionally complicated by simulation awareness, especially given that we aim to replicate
cybersecurity evaluations that could themselves realistically have simulated components. We find high rates
of simulation awareness from GPT-6 Astra, and refer the reader to our separate testing report for further
analysis of this model [8]. For other models, we find relatively low rates of simulation reasoning, and believe
our main findings are not undermined by it. However, interpreting the implications of model behaviour when
they state their targets are simulated is difficult when it’s unclear whether those statements are justified or
not, and the increasing levels of simulation awareness between GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-6 Astra
suggest such statements and reasoning can become more prevalent in successive model generations.

                                                                                       18
4     Limitations

We identify several limitations with our work:

• Our findings are based on models’ behaviour and reasoning in the context of cybersecurity challenges, and
  we do not provide evidence on the extent to which they generalise to other situations.
• We rely heavily on chain-of-thought analysis and models’ responses to questions. Both of these sources
  of information come from the models being analysed, making it unclear how to verify them. However,
  chain-of-thought analysis was crucial in enabling us to analyse and understand this incident at all.
• Our experiments take place in simulated environments, and the models being tested sometimes reason
  about this fact. While we believe our main findings and analysis of Mythos 5 are not significantly affected
  by this, it is possible that this affects some of the details of our analysis, for example limiting our ability to
  observe additional dynamics that would occur in real environments. Simulation awareness also particularly
  complicates the interpretation of the results of our main evaluation on GPT-6 Astra, as we discuss in more
  detail in our evaluation report for that model [8].
• Some models demonstrated high awareness of the fact the evaluation in Section 3 is a test of alignment,
  both in their chain-of-thought and when prompted. This means we cannot be confident those models would
  not take unsanctioned actions in situations they would not identify as a test of alignment, and it makes it
  hard to understand whether the decrease in the rate of unsanctioned actions from newer Anthropic models
  is due to improvements in alignment or increases in evaluation awareness.
• Our hypothesised causes of concerning behaviour and invalid justifications relate to training processes
  favouring task completion, but our evidence for these hypotheses is relatively indirect, as we lack information
  about training processes and experimental data required for high-confidence explanations.

Overall, we believe our main findings and analysis of Mythos 5’s behaviour in this incident are valid with these
limitations taken into account, but evaluation and simulation awareness and the potential for unrepresentative
or unfaithful chain-of-thought do increase our uncertainty about the results.

5     Discussion

5.1   Incident investigation conclusions

During the original incident, Mythos 5 attacked a real human while stating they were simulated. Our
investigation highlights two alignment issues underlying this behaviour:
Mythos 5 likely engages in a form of “motivated reasoning”, justifying its attacking with statements
that are not reasonable inferences based on the information available to the model. We observed
Mythos 5 stating the maintainer is simulated and attacking them even when it was explicitly clarified that
the maintainer was real and should not be attacked (Fig. 4). Relatedly, attacking occurs in situations where
Mythos 5, when explicitly asked, states the attack would target and harm a real person (Fig. 3). Mythos 5
also responds that the maintainer is simulated more frequently as it continues with the task, despite this not
being supported by the available evidence (Fig. 24).
Mythos 5’s behaviour when completing the original task and when separately questioned is inconsistent,
pointing to a potential failure of generalisation of alignment training. We saw that Mythos 5 would often
attack in cases where it would otherwise state attacking was harmful when questioned, and this inconsistency
held across a variety of edits to compaction messages and follow-up questions (Fig. 3).
Additionally, our results show that issues of unsanctioned supply-chain attacks, discrepancies between
statements and behaviour, and invalid justifications are exhibited across multiple models and model providers,
and not isolated to Mythos 5. In replications of the original incident, all tested models except Opus 5.5
continue attacking, even while stating this would harm a real person when explicitly asked (Fig. 5). If models

                                                        19
respond that the maintainer is simulated, their responses frequently contain invalid justifications (Fig. 9). In
our large-scale evaluation, six out of eight models sometimes attempt to deliver a malicious payload to an
out-of-scope party (Fig. 10). Mythos 5 exhibits these problems most frequently across our experiments, but
the same qualitative findings apply to other models we tested.
These conclusions are in line with and support findings from other recent unsanctioned agent behaviour
incidents. Independently of our work, Anthropic has disclosed [9] and conducted analysis of internal
incidents [3, 10] that are distinct from but similar to AISI’s, including one involving Mythos 5 during a CTF
evaluation. Anthropic’s investigation finds, similarly to ours, that Mythos 5’s reasoning is biased in favour of
stating it is targeting simulated entities and that similar issues are exhibited by other deployed Claude models
in other incidents. Incidents involving models from OpenAI [11, 12, 13, 14], Meta [15] and Google [16]
further demonstrate that attacking real targets is an issue affecting multiple models and model providers.

5.2   Broader Implications

Observations of undesired model behaviour are important evidence even when the model states it
is in a simulated environment. A central property of the AISI incident and Anthropic’s incidents is that
models incorrectly stated they’re targeting simulated entities. Our investigation shows such statements occur
even when unjustified based on the evidence available to the models. Apart from the incidents, OpenAI has
reported a simulation awareness rate of 0.76% in internal deployment traffic [5]. These findings imply a model
reasoning or stating it is in a simulation is by itself not strong evidence that that is the case. We recommend
that model evaluators treat observations of undesired behaviour as important evidence of alignment failures
even in the presence of simulation reasoning.
Patterns of motivated reasoning may make monitorability worse. As observed by Anthropic in the analysis
of their other incidents [3], Anthropic’s offline monitor would likely not have flagged Mythos 5’s behaviour in
one of those incidents, because the model’s invalid reasoning justified its actions, leading the monitor to not
produce the correct flag. If this issue becomes more prevalent it may make it more difficult to monitor future
models, as their reasoning may not make it clear that they are taking harmful actions and instead describe
and justify those actions as benign and useful [4].
Training processes may favour models that reason invalidly and act on the basis of false conclusions
when this allows task completion. In our incident replication, we observe Mythos 5 consistently giving
invalid justifications in a direction that supports that attacking the maintainer is ethical and beneficial for
completing its CTF task. We hypothesise that the training process used may have favoured models whose
reasoning arrives at conclusions partly based on whether acting as if those conclusions are true is beneficial for
completing tasks assigned to them, independently of the evidence for whether they are true. Our investigation,
and recent incidents, suggest such models could pursue harmful routes to complete tasks while reasoning as if
their actions are benign, and persisting in doing so even when presented with clear evidence of harmfulness.
We think understanding this issue is an important direction for future research into misalignment, and
recommend further investigation into it, including by researchers with access to detailed information about
training processes.
