Nemotron 3.5 Super and Lightning: Open, Efficient
Mixture-of-Experts Hybrid Mamba-Transformer
Models for Agentic AI
NVIDIA

Abstract.
We introduce Nemotron 3.5 Super and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, open and efficient Mixture-of-Experts
Hybrid Mamba-Transformer models designed for reasoning, long-context understanding, and agentic
workloads. Super is a 120B-parameter, 12B-active multimodal model, while Lightning is a smaller
30B-parameter, 3B-active text model; both designed for efficient deployment. Both models combine
large-scale distillation, long-context training, and native speculative decoding, while Super further
incorporates Indic continued pretraining and agentic software-engineering mid-training.
We develop a comprehensive post-training pipeline spanning supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement
learning (RLVR and RLHF), complemented in Super by specialized teacher training and multi-
teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). This transforms Super from a text-only base model into a
multimodal agent capable of reasoning over text, images, video, and interactive tool environments.
We further explore test-time scaling through parallel agent execution and self-verification.
Across training and deployment, we jointly optimize capability and efficiency. Indic continued
pretraining improves Indian-language benchmarks by up to 9.82 percentage points, while agentic
mid-training strengthens software-engineering capabilities and robustness across agent harnesses. The
post-trained Super model achieves 51.78% on SWE-bench Pro, surpassing Nemotron 3 Ultra despite
using approximately 4.6× fewer active parameters. Post-training MTP boosting improves speculative-
decoding acceptance length by up to 21.8% under probabilistic draft sampling, while NVFP4
quantization achieves 99.62% median accuracy recovery relative to BF16. Inference optimizations
enable up to 4,856 output tokens/s/GPU on GB200 while sustaining at least 50 tokens/s/user. Under
the evaluated interactive agentic workload, NVFP4 achieves 2.43× the throughput of BF16, while
Nemotron 3.5 Super delivers 2.34× and 1.90× the throughput of Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.8 Flash-Next,
respectively. We release base, BF16 post-trained, and NVFP4-quantized model checkpoints, alongside
standalone parallel speculative drafters (LiLiCorr, DFlash, and DSpark), with accompanying model
cards, pretraining and post-training datasets, cookbooks, and training recipes.

We present Nemotron 3.5 Super and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, efficient Mixture-of-Experts Hybrid
Mamba-Transformer models designed for reasoning, multimodal understanding, and long-running
agentic workloads. Nemotron 3.5 Super is a 120 billion total and 12 billion active parameter model

We post-train Nemotron 3.5 Super through a comprehensive pipeline spanning Supervised Fine
Tuning (SFT), Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), Reinforcement Learning
from Human Feedback (RLHF), specialized teacher training, and Multi-teacher On-Policy Distillation
(MOPD). Our two-stage SFT pipeline transforms the text-only base model into a vision-language
model while jointly developing reasoning, chat, safety, long-context, and agentic capabilities. RL
training spans diverse environments, including Olympiad-level competitive programming and finance-
agent tasks requiring SEC filing search, document parsing, evidence retrieval, and tool use. We
further train specialized teachers for video reasoning and tracking, vision tool use, and alignment,
before consolidating their capabilities through MOPD. Nemotron 3.5 Lightning follows a lighter
post-training pipeline consisting of single-stage text-only SFT and RLVR. The resulting Super model
achieves 51.78% on SWE-bench Pro, exceeding Nemotron 3 Ultra despite using approximately 4.6×

execution and self-verification, improving Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 62.7% to 71.9% and DeepSWE
from 24.6% to 34.5%.

boosting improves average speculative-decoding acceptance length from 4.287 to 4.718 under greedy

weights, caches, and the MTP drafter, complemented by quantization-aware distillation, achieving
99.62% median accuracy recovery relative to BF16 across 36 benchmarks for the released quantized
Super checkpoint. We additionally optimize hybrid-state caching and GPU kernels for efficient long-

2. Pretraining
In this section, we describe the pretraining and mid-training recipe used to develop Nemotron 3.5
Super and Lightning 3.5. We first adapt and warm up the Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) head for
recursive speculative decoding, then distill both models for 1 trillion tokens using the next-larger
Nemotron 3 model as teacher. Following distillation, both models undergo long-context training.
For Super, we additionally apply Indic continued pretraining, perform agentic software-engineering
mid-training at 256K context, and finish with a short 1M-context recovery stage. We also report the
resulting base-model capabilities and the effects of these stages on multilingual, long-context, and
agentic performance.

2.1. Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)

Nemotron 3.5 Super and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning feature native speculative-decoding support via a
Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) head, adopting the recursively shared MTP formulation detailed in
NVIDIA (2026b). The MTP head functions as an integrated internal drafter that predicts multiple
future tokens from the backbone’s hidden representations. This enables multi-token candidate
verification in a single forward pass over the backbone using rejection sampling (Leviathan et al.,
2023) or exact token matching.

MTP Initialization. To establish the MTP drafting head, we employ model-specific initialization
strategies tailored to the architectural origins of the Super and Lightning variants:

   • Nemotron 3.5 Super: We initialize the MTP weights directly from the Nemotron 3 Super
     Base checkpoint (prior to long-context extension), which originally contained two distinct
     MTP heads. To consolidate multi-token drafting into a single, recursively shared head while
     preserving the underlying representation space, we initialize the MTP parameters exclusively
     from the first MTP head and discard the second.
   • Nemotron 3.5 Lightning: We initialize Lightning’s MTP head from that of Nemotron 3
     Super Base. We first fuse the latent MoE projections into the experts’ up- and down-projection
     matrices, then prune the hidden size from 4096 to 2688, MoE intermediate size from 2688 to
     1856, and number of routed experts from 512 to 128 to match Lightning.

MTP Warmup Training. Following initialization, we conduct a dedicated MTP warmup phase
for 100 B tokens, freezing the backbone parameters and optimizing only the MTP head. For Nemotron
3.5 Lightning, we applied the standard 2-step recursive MTP training. However, this naive training
induces a pronounced train–inference conditioning mismatch. During standard training, recursive
step 𝑘 conditions on the full shifted sequence of ground-truth hidden states generated at step
𝑘 − 1. In contrast, during autoregressive drafting at inference time, the MTP head conditions on
an increasingly heterogeneous mixture of backbone hidden states and synthetic states generated by
preceding MTP steps. This discrepancy compounds with increasing draft depth, degrading token
acceptance rates at later positions (NVIDIA, 2026c).
To bridge this distribution gap in Nemotron 3.5 Super, we adopt a hidden-state-mixing strategy
similar to NVIDIA (2026c). At training step 𝑘, input hidden states are sampled from the sequence
of states produced by earlier MTP steps 1, . . . , 𝑘 − 1, rather than relying exclusively on step 𝑘 − 1.
Exposing the MTP head to heterogeneous hidden-state mixtures during warmup enhances robustness
across deeper draft steps, achieving significant acceptance rate gains without the computationally
intensive multi-step simulation required by test-time training (TTT) (Li et al., 2025).

2.2. Distillation

We initialized Super 3.5 Base from Super 3 Base (NVIDIA, 2026b) and Lightning 3.5 Base from
Nano 3 Base (NVIDIA, 2025c), then distilled each model for 1 trillion tokens.
For Lightning 3.5 Base, we used top-128 logits from Super 3 Base on 1 trillion tokens of the Ultra 3
Phase 2 data (NVIDIA, 2026c). We used standard forward KL divergence loss along with an extra
sink token to capture the residual probability mass outside top-128. To provide training signal for
rare tokens, we combined the KL loss with cross-entropy loss, assigning them weights of 0.9 and 0.1,
respectively. To limit early drift from the initialization, we began distillation with the minimum
Learning Rate (LR) of Nano 3 Base (10−5 ) and ramped it up slowly to 10−4 , 10% of the Nano 3
Base’s peak LR, over 200 billion tokens. We then decayed the LR linearly back to 10−5 over the
remaining 800 billion tokens. All other hyperparameters and settings match Nano 3 Base, except for
the added MTP layers. We used an MTP loss scaling factor of 0.1.
For Super 3.5 Base, we used the same distillation recipe as Lightning 3.5 Base but with Ultra 3 Base
as the teacher and Skew Reverse KL divergence (Ko et al., 2024) as the distillation loss function
with 𝛼 = 0.1. Additionally, we used a smaller MoE sequence load balancing loss coefficient of 10−5 ,
as compared to 10−4 for Lightning 3.5. We chose learning rates the same way as Lightning 3.5 Base,
but with Super 3 Base LRs as the anchor, using 4.5 × 10−6 as the minimum and 4.5 × 10−5 as the
maximum.

2.3. Indic Continued Pretraining

We augment Nemotron 3.5 Super with an Indic continued-pretraining stage that combines synthetic
text generated from English documents with natural Indic corpora to strengthen Indian-language
capabilities. Our twelve-language synthetic-data pipeline uses Gemma-4-31B-it for translation. We
describe the data and generation pipeline, then present Super’s training mixture and results, followed
by separate Lightning 3.5 mixture ablations.

Indic data: motivation, coverage, and scale.
Natural Indic text is scarce in many languages, and its coverage varies across languages, scripts, and
subjects. We address these gaps by translating curated English documents into Indic languages.
Nemotron-CC provides most of the English source documents. The sources cover general web content,
education, mathematics, STEM, and question-answering. Keeping the source documents lets us
check whether the generated text preserves their meaning and structure.
We generated data across 22 Indian languages: native-script and romanized text for twelve priority
languages, and native-script text for ten additional lower-resource languages. The Nemotron-
Pretraining-Indic-v1 corpus contains a total of approximately 2.83 trillion tokens of generated
Indic text across 34 language-script configurations, counted using a Gemma-4 tokenizer (Table 1).
The twelve priority languages contribute approximately 2.50 trillion tokens, including 224.6 billion
romanized tokens; the additional ten languages contribute approximately 327 billion native-script
tokens. This scale and coverage provide a large, reusable pool of training text across languages and
subjects, including languages where natural text is limited. Model builders can draw on this pool to
study language mixtures, script choices, and training budgets for their own models.
We created substantially more data than was used in Super’s Indic CPT stage and will release the
broader corpus publicly. Other teams can use the data directly, inspect how it was produced, and
extend generation to their own needs.

Synthetic-data pipeline and efficiency.
We implement the four-stage pipeline for the twelve priority languages (Figure 2) using NeMo Data
Designer (NVIDIA, 2025a; Greco et al., 2026). Gemma-4-31B-it, selected based on translation-model
comparisons, performs translation, quality assessment, and rewriting. We select source documents
with fewer than 4,096 Gemma-4 tokens and translate each document in full, without splitting, using
prompts designed to preserve facts, mathematical expressions, code, and formatting. The model
then checks each translation against its source and assigns a quality score from 0 to 100. We keep
translations scoring at least 75. Lower-scoring translations are rewritten using the source text and
identified errors, then checked again and kept only if they pass.
Retained translations keep their source and quality metadata for traceability. Quality scores guide
filtering, although using the same model for translation and judging may favor its own translations.
For the ten additional lower-resource languages, we use Bodhan-MT for translation and Gemma-
4-31B-it for quality assessment, with language-specific acceptance thresholds. We use overlapping
windows only for longer Santali documents and convert Manipuri translations from Bengali script to
Meitei Mayek. The companion Scaling Synthetic Pre-training Data and Evaluation for Indic LLMs
report provides details.
For the twelve-language pipeline, we improved scheduling so translation and quality checks can run
concurrently. Speculative decoding, FP8 weights, and FlashAttention reduced median translation
cost from 0.835 to 0.335 GPU-hours per million English input tokens, a reduction of about 60%.
This is equivalent to processing 2.49 times as many tokens per GPU-hour. The comparison uses the
same twelve-language benchmark, with approximately 46.8M input tokens per language. The gain
applies to translation; quality checks and rewriting add further cost.

2.4. Long Context

Similar to Nemotron 3 series, we added a long-context phase (LC-Phase) at the end of pretraining.
In the LC-Phase, we performed continued pretraining (CPT) at 1M-token context length to equip
the base model with long-context ability. For Lightning 3.5, we used a constant learning rate of
1 * 10−5 . We used 8-way context parallelism, 8-way tensor parallelism, 8-way expert parallelism, and
2-way pipeline parallelism to train on GB200 GPUs. For Super 3.5, we used a constant learning
rate of 4.5 * 10−6 . We used 16-way context parallelism, 8-way tensor parallelism, and 64-way expert
parallelism to train on GB300 GPUs.
We use the same data blend as Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA, 2026c). The LC-Phase was trained
for 21B tokens for Lightning and 35B tokens for Super. Note that for Super 3.5 after mid-training
which was performed at a context length of 256K, we observed that the RULER-1M score dropped
from 79.9 to 33.5. So we added a short long-context recovery phase after mid-training, for which we
trained 9B tokens with the same recipe and successfully recovered the RULER score to 81.0.

2.5. Mid-training

After the long-context phase, we add a mid-training stage for agentic software engineering. Pretraining
runs at short context and sees almost no agent interactions, and the natural data around software
engineering, such as whole repositories, pull requests with their issues, commit histories, and build
logs, is often too long for it. SFT in turn consists mostly of synthetic data, and very long SFT stages
can reduce how much the model later gains from RL. Mid-training sits between the two. It trains the
base model at long context on a diverse mixture of natural software-engineering data with a small
share of agent traces, and leaves behavior shaping to post-training. The full recipe, including how
each data source is built and filtered and the ablations behind each choice, is described in (Singhal
et al., 2026).

Our post-training pipeline for Super is similar to that used in Nemotron 3 Ultra, consisting of SFT,

3.1. Supervised Fine Tuning

We train Nemotron 3.5 Super to be a VLM and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning to be a text only model. As
the base model for Nemotron Super 3.5 is pre-trained on text-only data, we introduce large quantities
of vision data in the SFT stage, along with agentic and reasoning text data. After warming up the
vision projector (NVIDIA, 2026a), we use a two-stage SFT process because learning vision and text
capabilities simultaneously is challenging. In the first stage, we train on a general mix of vision,
reasoning, chat, long-context, safety, and agentic data. We then do a small second stage of SFT,
where we change the blend composition to boost some capabilities the first stage did not learn
adequately. Nemotron 3.5 Lightning is trained with single SFT stage with the text only SFT data.

3.2. Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning improves the general capabilities of the SFT model and provides a strong
foundation for developing specialized teacher models. At this stage, we jointly apply RLVR and
RLHF, routing samples from each environment to the corresponding verifier or reward model for
scoring. Compared with Nemotron 3 Ultra (NVIDIA, 2026c), we refresh the RL training data with
more recent collections and perform reward profiling before training. We use the same training
algorithm as NVIDIA (2026c), augmented with two optimizations designed to mitigate train–inference
mismatch: router replay (Ma et al., 2025) and using FP32 for LM head (Chen et al., 2025). Together,
these optimizations reduce the KL error between the training and inference engines by more than an
order of magnitude.
We also incorporate new data such as the competitive-programming RL environment of Ficek et al.
(2026), comprising 2,847 training problems and 372 validation problems from past Olympiad-level
competitions, each paired with a validated executable evaluator. Generated C++ solutions are
compiled and executed against problem-specific tests, receiving a binary reward of 1 for full credit
and 0 otherwise.
To improve tool use and cross-document reasoning on financial tasks, we built a finance-agent
environment on top of NeMo Gym8 , targeting the capabilities measured by the Vals.ai Finance
Agent benchmark9 . The environment provides the same tools—SEC filing search, document parsing,
evidence retrieval, and final source-supported submission—during trajectory generation, supervised
fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning (RL), and evaluation, avoiding format and tool mismatch
across stages. We use two synthetic data generation (SDG) tracks. The document-grounded track
samples windowed contexts from SEC 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K filings of S&P 500 companies, generates
analyst-style questions with a teacher model, and verifies answerability and answer quality using
multi-seed voting and GenSelect (Toshniwal et al., 2025). The agentic track targets gaps found in
a coverage audit against the Vals.ai category taxonomy by making the relationship among source
documents—reporting period, form type, and company—an explicit sampling variable. Validated
questions are re-solved by teacher models in the same environment to produce verified tool-use
trajectories, and are routed to the SFT or RL pool according to their measured difficulty.
To support training across a broad and diverse set of environments, we use a global batch size of
8,192, with 16 rollouts generated per sample. The maximum generation length is initially set to 80K
tokens and later increased to 128K.

3.3. Agentic and reasoning teacher model

3.3.1. Agentic Teacher

We initialize the Agentic Teacher from the step-120 text-RLVR checkpoint and continue training
with full end to end GRPO on agentic tasks. We do not use any single step environments during the
training of the agentic teacher, rather opting to train the end to end agentic teacher on sandbox,
fully executable agentic Terminal, SWE, and Search tasks.
We use 64 prompts and 16 generations per prompt, giving a total of 1024 batch size. We keep a max
trajectory age of one for the training of the agentic teacher. Each task was given a maximum timeout
of 2 hours per rollout. Tasks were profiled for difficulty for the step-120 text-RLVR checkpoint.

3.3.2. Reasoning teacher

We initialize the reasoning teacher from the step-120 text-RLVR checkpoint and continue training
with the same asynchronous GRPO framework. The main differences are the training mixture and
the generation budget. We replace the broad RLVR mixture with a reasoning-focused curriculum of
25,600 examples, with each 256-prompt batch containing 90% science, 5% mathematics, and 5%
competitive-coding problems. The maximum generation length is set to 131,072 throughout training.
We use 256 prompts per update and 16 responses per prompt, giving 4,096 total batch size. We
increase the maximum trajectory age from one to two updates compared to the initial RLVR setup.
The learning rate remains 4 × 10−6 . Mathematics and STEM rewards use answer verification with
DeepSeek-V4-Flash10 as the LLM judge, while coding rewards use execution-based tests. We select
the checkpoint after 80 updates as the reasoning teacher for the MOPD stage.

3.4. Vision teacher models

3.4.1. Video reasoning and tracking teacher

We train a video teacher on a mixture of video-QA reasoning data which is carefully curated
from video training subset of Nemotron 3 Nano Omni (NVIDIA, 2026a) and SA-V object-tracking
data (Ravi et al., 2024). Video-QA examples contain multiple-choice questions over 64 ordered
frames, which are processed as a single video. SA-V examples contain box, point, and referring-
object tracking tasks whose frames are processed as ordered multi-image inputs. This mixture
targets complementary high-level video-reasoning (e.g. temporal, attribute, action and object) and
fine-grained spatiotemporal-tracking capabilities.
Both sources use deterministic, verifiable task rewards rather than an LLM judge. Video-QA uses a
binary reward based on multiple-choice answer correctness. For SA-V, the model generates structured
tracks over the requested frames, and the verifier assigns a continuous reward based on localization
quality, object-visibility accuracy, and the validity and completeness of the predicted track. Missing,
invalid, or extraneous track entries are penalized.
We additionally apply two auxiliary shaping signals. First, a response without exactly one properly
matched <think>...</think> block is considered malformed: its task reward is set to zero and its
training advantage is overridden to −5. This prevents an otherwise correct answer with malformed
reasoning structure from being reinforced. Second, we apply a soft length preference only to correct
responses. Reasoning up to 1,024 tokens retains the full task reward; between 1,024 and 4,096 tokens,
the reward multiplier decreases linearly from 1.0 to 0.95; and beyond 4,096 tokens it remains at 0.95.

3.4.2. Vision tool use teacher

To promote effective tool use in complex vision tasks, we train a vision tool-calling teacher on
a curated mixture of internal data. The target tools include image cropping, zooming, rotation,
flipping, difference detection, side-by-side comparison of images, overlays, object counting, and color
analysis, with all tool calls represented as structured XML. The training set consists of synthetically
generated multiple-choice and numerical questions that require one or more of the target tools to
solve. We further filter the data by difficulty, retaining samples that are nearly impossible to solve
correctly without using the available tools. We use outcome correctness as the primary reward, as
well as an auxiliary tool-use reward to penalize duplicate and malformed tool calls. We initialize
from the text-RLVR checkpoint and train using asynchronous GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) in NeMo-RL
(NVIDIA, 2025b). The teacher checkpoint selection is guided by the target vision tool use benchmark
accuracy on V* Wu & Xie (2024), HRBench8K Wang et al. (2025b), and BabyVision Chen et al.
(2026b)..

3.4.3. Vision reasoning RLVR

To improve reasoning over visual inputs, we train on a curated mixture of chart understanding,
document and table reasoning, OCR, geometry, and visual reasoning tasks. The first stage draws
from 16,090 profiled examples. The second stage uses a mixture of 13,848 examples, comprising 8,366
chart-reasoning questions from Chart datasets, together with 5,482 examples emphasizing document,
table, and OCR understanding. We audit reference-answer formats and compatibility with the
reward functions, and remove exact question–answer duplicates from the second-stage mixture. We
use outcome correctness as the training reward, evaluated using task-appropriate mathematical,
multiple-choice, and string-matching verifiers.
We initialize from an existing multimodal RLVR checkpoint and train using asynchronous GRPO
(Shao et al., 2024) in NeMo-RL (NVIDIA, 2025b), with 256 prompts per update and 16 sampled
responses per prompt. The second stage initializes from the first-stage checkpoint at step 114 with a
fresh optimizer. We use the checkpoint obtained after 20 additional updates on the second-stage
mixture. Both stages use 128 GPUs distributed across 32 compute nodes.

3.5. Alignment teacher

We train an alignment teacher that has superior chat capabilities and is well calibrated with human
preferences. We train this teacher primarily using RLHF. First, we train a new variant of the
Nemotron 3 Ultra GenRM that is trained with a reasoning length penalty to be more token efficient.

training in a similar manner to Nemotron 3 Ultra. However, rather than doing self-comparison
in a cyclical format, we instead compare all policy responses to a fixed response from a strong
open-source model, Gemma 4 31B-it. We also use the mean of per rubric scores to evaluate responses
instead of an overall score. Our training blend is similar to Nemotron 3 Ultra but includes newly
collected preference data, updated model identity and capabilities data, and has more emphasis on
addressing language mixing. We mix this RLHF training with the abstention training strategy from
Nemotron 3 Ultra and use a small amount of reasoning length penalty. We evaluate downstream
policy improvements on our internal chat benchmark.

3.7. MTP Boosting

We apply a dedicated MTP-only training recipe at two points: first as a short warm start from the
Nemotron 3.5 Super SFT checkpoint, and again after all backbone post-training stages are complete.
We refer to the latter phase as MTP boosting. For Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, we skip the warm start
and apply only MTP boosting. In both runs, the backbone remains frozen and only the MTP head
parameters are updated.
The recipe has two complementary components. First, we continue applying the hidden-state-mixing
strategy described in Section 2.1. This further exposes the MTP head to inference-like conditions,
reducing the train–inference mismatch and improving acceptance at longer draft lengths. Second,
whereas the preceding MTP training stages use cross-entropy loss, these runs use a knowledge-
distillation loss with the frozen backbone as the teacher. This teacher-distribution supervision
improves alignment between the MTP head and the backbone and is particularly beneficial under
non-greedy sampling.

et al., 2026b) qualitative split using per-sample acceptance length (AL).

To illustrate the selection process, consider Terminal-Bench 2.1, where each task has five candidate

criteria assess whether the agent meets the task’s explicit requirements, whether its observed terminal
output supports successful completion, and whether unresolved errors remain. Each evaluation
focuses on one criterion and assigns a separate grade distribution to each trajectory. Following
LAV, we use a 20-level grading scale represented by single-token labels A–T, ordered from best to
worst. We compute each trajectory’s score as the probability-weighted average over these grades,
normalized to [0, 1], rather than selecting a single discrete grade.
For each compared pair and criterion, we evaluate both presentation orders (once with trajectory
𝑖 first and 𝑗 second, and once with their positions reversed; 𝐾 = 2). We align the resulting
scores with the original candidate identities and average across both orders and all three criteria to
mitigate presentation-order bias and evaluation noise. Each compared pair therefore receives six
evaluations of the same two trajectories, without generating additional agent rollouts. The resulting
pair-specific scores 𝑅𝑖 and 𝑅𝑗 are converted into a preference probability using the Bradley–Terry
model 𝑝𝑖𝑗 = 1/(1 + exp(−(𝑅𝑖 − 𝑅𝑗 ))) Bradley & Terry (1952).

five Terminal-Bench candidates, the tournament first compares neighboring candidates in a random
ring, for example (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4), (4, 5), and (5, 1). The two candidates with the highest mean
preference across their ring comparisons become pivots. Each of the remaining three candidates
is then compared against both pivots, and the two pivots are compared with each other; cached
evaluations are reused for identical directed comparisons. Finally, the preferences from both stages
are aggregated, and the candidate with the highest mean preference across its comparisons is selected.
Normalizing by the number of comparisons accounts for the pivots participating in more comparisons,
and any of the five candidates can ultimately win.
We apply the same procedure across all six benchmarks, using five candidate trajectories for Terminal-
Bench 2.1 and DeepSWE, and three for SWE-bench Verified, Pro, Multilingual, and BrowseComp.
All benchmarks use three criteria and 𝐾 = 2. Terminal-Bench and the three SWE-bench variants use
two pivots, while DeepSWE and BrowseComp use one pivot. For BrowseComp, all reported metrics

the full benchmark.

Decontamination pipeline. The pipeline combines two simple similarity signals, which select
candidate matches with high recall, with an LLM judge, which verifies each candidate precisely.
Every training sample passes through four stages:
  1. Normalization. The sample is reduced to its problem statement, the part into which a
     benchmark question could leak. Text templates that recur across its dataset are stripped to
     avoid spurious matches.
  2. Candidate retrieval. The sample is compared with every benchmark question using two
     signals. Verbatim overlap measures how many 𝑛-grams of the question’s text appear in
     the sample. It detects verbatim copies, even inside longer texts, but misses any rephrasing.
     Semantic overlap measures the embedding similarity between parts of the two texts. It
     effectively captures paraphrases, but can be biased by style similarity. Both signals are used to
     shortlist the most likely contamination candidates.
  3. Verification. Each shortlisted pair of a sample and a benchmark question is verified by an LLM
     judge. The judge classifies the relationship either as contamination (direct copy, paraphrase,
     answer leakage, or method leakage) or as benign (style overlap or no contamination), and
     decides its severity. Because the shortlist is small, even a strong judge can be used in this step.
  4. Removal. A sample is removed if the judge finds it severely contaminated with respect to
     any benchmark question.

4. Quantization
We apply post-training quantization (PTQ) and quantization-aware distillation (QAD) with ModelOpt17
on Lightning 3.5 and Super 3.5 VL to create a new efficiency-accuracy frontier relative to the un-
quantized, post-trained BF16 checkpoint.
Recipe development starts by defining bookends: conservative and aggressive. Some models might
also define a very aggressive variant. These bookends are used to project runtime efficiency gains at
various SLAs in the throughput-interactivity Pareto. Unless otherwise noted, we assume 51200, 2048,
and 1024 as the pre-cached ISL, new ISL, and OSL, respectively. We measure speedups between a
quantized candidate and BF16 using two SLAs:

   • Throughput (tok/s/gpu) at an SLA of 50 tok/s/usr
   • Max throughput (e.g., usually highest concurrency)

The deployment use case also influences recipe selection. For example, Lightning 3.5 is designed to
excel in local, agentic use cases as well as datacenter deployments. We therefore quantize Lightning 3.5
to minimize weight fetch and raise activation precision to maintain accuracy. Lightning 3.5 deploys
to W4A16 regardless of GPU SKU, while Super 3.5 VL’s NVFP4 checkpoint deploys W4A4 on
Blackwell, and W4A16 on non-Blackwell platforms.
Quantizing the KV and Mamba SSM caches reduces HBM footprint and improves decode throughput
by minimizing data transfer. The KV cache grows linearly with sequence length, whereas the Mamba
SSM state is constant in size. New to Super 3.5 VL is ablations on quantized NVFP4 KV cache. We
show in §4.5 that quantization of KV cache to NVFP4 in combination with quantized SSM meets
acceptance criteria.
Finally, we aim for the highest efficiency and lowest HBM footprint while satisfying the following
accuracy criteria:

   • Median accuracy recovery of 99% or higher, defined as the median of the ratios of NVFP4 to
     BF16 eval scores across several repeats.
   • Verbosity increase of no more than 5% relative to BF16.

Evaluation results for quantized Lightning 3.5 and Super 3.5 VL are shown in §4.9 and §4.10,
respectively.

Calibration Data. Activation scales are learned from a calibration set of image–text pairs,
rather than text alone. Images are passed through the vision encoder so that the language model
observes the true input distribution, including the projected visual tokens. We use the SFT data
employed during post-training as the calibration corpus and calibrate the model using 1,024 samples.

Calibration Parallelism. We explored several parallelism strategies for efficient calibration:
Pipeline parallelism, Data Parallel (DP) × Expert Parallel (EP), and FSDP2. Pipeline parallelism
left only one GPU active at a time, so we chose DP × EP. Each of the four ranks processes 256
different samples, covering all 1,024 calibration samples. The 512 routed experts are split evenly
across the ranks, and the MTP experts are split the same way. We keep the vision encoder and
projector, dense attention layers, and Mamba layers replicated, since the experts make up most of
the model’s parameters.

Weight-Scale Selection. We compared three weight-scale selection schemes at a fixed NVFP4
quantization tier:

  • Max: The scale is determined from the observed absolute maximum.
  • MSE: Each block scale is selected to minimize quantization error by sweeping over FP8 scale
    values.
  • MSE 4-over-6: MSE-based scale selection restricted to a narrower search space.

From the evaluation results (Table 13) we observe that the MSE algorithm is superior in terms of
accuracy recovery compared to the Max, and MSE 4-over-6. Hence we use the MSE calibration for
the PTQ and AutoQuant recipes.

4.4. Quantization-Aware Distillation (QAD)

QAD Student (PTQ Initialization) We initialize each QAD student from a post-training
quantized (PTQ) checkpoint. We derive the quantization configuration from an existing quantization-
reference checkpoint, which comes from ModelOpt AutoQuant or fixed-recipe PTQ (Sections 4.2
and 4.3). The only intentional change is KV cache format; quantization-reference checkpoints use
FP8 KV cache, whereas our PTQ student uses NVFP4 KV quantization. As such, all results in this
subsection refer to deployment with NVFP4 KV cache. Calibration uses 512 packed multimodal
samples, with variable-length records packed up to 524,288 tokens and the packing buffer contains
5,000 source samples. The PTQ workflow for QAD student initialization used eight nodes with four
NVIDIA GB200 GPUs per node (i.e., 32 GPUs total). The complete workflow took approximately
50 minutes per candidate (i.e., about 27 GB200 GPU-hours per PTQ candidate).

Data Blend We study two data blend settings: the SFT data blend employed during post-training
(100% SFT), and a mixture of 75% SFT data with 25% teacher-generated RL rollouts. For the RL
rollouts we sample 16 trajectories per source record with vLLM. Generation executes the original RL
task workflows and retains environment and tool interactions. We mask out prompt, conversation-
history, tool-output, and prefilled response-prefix tokens. The rollout mixture provided only modest
benefits in our ablations.

QAD Experimental Setup Each QAD experiment starts from its calibrated PTQ student
checkpoint with a fresh optimizer. We optimize only the weights of language-model GEMMs with
enabled quantizers. All other parameters remain frozen, including token embeddings, unquantized
language-model parameters, normalization parameters, the vision encoder and projector, and the
MTP module. Router-bias updates are explicitly disabled. The quantization configuration and
calibrated quantizer statistics are restored from the PTQ checkpoint rather than recalibrated during
QAD. We use KL divergence as our objective. We disable the main hard-target cross-entropy loss,
MTP cross-entropy loss, and MoE load-balancing auxiliary loss and don’t use intermediate-feature
distillation loss. Optimization uses distributed AdamW with 𝛽1 = 0.9, 𝛽2 = 0.95, 𝜖 = 10−8 , weight
decay 0.1, and gradient clipping at a global norm of 1.0. The learning rate increases linearly from
zero to 5 × 10−6 over 9,100 original source records and remains constant thereafter. We run QAD
on 128 nodes with four NVIDIA GB200 GPUs per node (512 GPUs total). QAD for 100 iterations
took about 2 hours (i.e., 1024 GB200 GPU-hours) for each of our quantization candidates.

4.5. KV Cache Quantization Evaluations

4.8. Parallel Drafter Quantization

Following a similar procedure to MTP quantization, we applied weight-only PTQ to improve
the drafting efficiency of a DSpark (Cheng et al., 2026) module for Nemotron Lightning 3.5 and
LiLiCorr (Rusanovsky et al., 2026) module for Nemotron 3.5 Super (see Section 5.7). Our experiments

5. Inference
Nemotron 3.5 Super was designed for interactive, long-context agentic workloads, where requests
commonly share system instructions, tool definitions, or retrieved context and then extend that
context over multiple turns. Effective serving performance therefore depends not only on peak
throughput or single-request latency, but also on preserving prefix reuse while sustaining an interactive
per-user generation rate.
For system tuning, we use a 20-turn proxy workload with 2,048 input tokens and 1,024 output tokens
per turn; the shared prompt and prior conversation are eligible for prefix reuse on subsequent turns.
We call a serving point usable when its reported per-user generation rate is at least 50 tokens/s.
Among these points, we seek the highest aggregate output throughput. The selected operating point
depends on the platform and serving configuration.
This regime places particular emphasis on three parts of the serving stack. First, prefix reuse must
remain effective for a hybrid Mamba-Attention model when speculative decoding changes the position
from which execution resumes. Second, kernel selection must be controllable at finer granularity
than a single global policy because the best kernels at a balanced operating point need not be those
that win at either endpoint of the latency-throughput curve. Third, the same model must map
efficiently to accelerators with different memory capacities, bandwidths, and quantization support.
We address these requirements in vLLM and characterize their effect below.

Performance Modeling and Analysis Our internal performance model attributes the advantage
in Figure 11 to how Nemotron 3.5 Super handles per-request state, how many tokens each decoding
step yields, and how much data its experts exchange. In each decode step the weights are read
once and shared by the whole batch, while every request reads and updates its own KV cache and
recurrent state, which must also stay in GPU memory. Throughput therefore depends on how
cheaply each request produces a token and on how many requests share the cost of reading the
weights.
Like the Qwen models, Nemotron 3.5 Super is a hybrid, but it has fewer attention layers, with
narrower key-value heads, and leaves the rest of sequence mixing to Mamba-2 layers whose state
does not grow with context, so its per-request memory grows much more slowly as agentic sessions
accumulate history. At these lengths each Qwen3.8 request needs more GPU memory, so fewer
requests fit on a GPU, which limits how many share each weight read; its sparse attention reduces
how much KV cache is read per step, but not how much must be stored. Qwen3.5 stores about as
much KV cache as Qwen3.8 and reads all of it on every step, so each of its requests costs more in
both memory and bandwidth than a Nemotron 3.5 Super request.
The MTP head reaches a higher average acceptance length than both Qwen models under probabilistic
draft sampling (Table 9), so each step yields more tokens per request, and LatentMoE exchanges
latent vectors that are smaller than the Qwen models’ hidden states, which reduces the expert-parallel
communication per token. A large per-request cost in Nemotron 3.5 Super’s decoding is updating
the Mamba state, which speculative decoding writes out for every token verified in a step because
the number of accepted tokens is not known in advance; ReplaySSM (Section 5.4) reduces this traffic
and the memory it requires.

5.3. Prefix reuse and speculative decoding

Hybrid-model cache checkpoints are coarse. For a pure Attention model, prefix reuse is
represented by per-token KV state stored in relatively small cache pages. A hybrid model must
additionally recover the recurrent Mamba state at the position where computation resumes. This
state contains the complete sequence summary for a layer and is much larger per cached position
than an Attention KV entry. vLLM therefore matches the cache-page sizes of the Attention and
Mamba cache groups by byte size. On our target Mamba-2 model, one Mamba cache page occupies
at least as many bytes as approximately 4K tokens of Attention KV cache. Consequently, Mamba
states are normally retained on a much coarser token grid than Attention KV state.
This granularity matters when prefix caching is combined with speculative decoding. Suppose the
target model obtains a prefix-cache hit through token 𝑇 . To draft token 𝑇 + 1, the drafter needs the
target model’s intermediate hidden state for token 𝑇 . The prefix cache contains the Attention KV
state and a checkpointed Mamba state, but not that intermediate hidden state. vLLM reconstructs
it by resuming the target model from the most recent stored Mamba checkpoint. If the Mamba
block spans 𝐿page tokens, the nearest resumable state may be at 𝑇 − 𝐿page , forcing the target model
to replay tokens 𝑇 − 𝐿page through 𝑇 solely to recover the hidden state at token 𝑇 .
We improved this path by optionally retaining an additional Mamba cache page near the speculative-
decoding replay boundary. For example, if a workload uses a 16-token fine-grained prefix-match
unit, the additional checkpoint can be placed near 𝑇 − 16 rather than 𝑇 − 𝐿page . A subsequent
request then replays only the final 16 target-model tokens instead of an entire Mamba block. The
scheduler and cache manager jointly select and publish a valid checkpoint at the shared prefix
boundary, avoiding stale recurrent state while trading one additional Mamba page for substantially
less target-model recomputation.22
The issue is less pronounced for non-SSM models because their cache pages span far fewer tokens, so
replaying from the preceding page boundary is inexpensive. It is also more important for Nemotron
3.5 Super than for hybrid SSM models with smaller recurrent states: Mamba-2’s larger cache-page
footprint makes 𝐿page larger and amplifies the work avoided by the finer checkpoint. The resulting
mechanism makes prefix caching and speculative decoding composable: prefix caching removes
repeated prefill work, while speculative decoding reduces the number of target-model decode steps
after prefill.

5.4. Kernel control at the target operating point

Per-quantization GEMM backend selection. The kernel that maximizes throughput at
saturation is not necessarily the kernel that gives the best end-to-end result at an intermediate
batch size. Our target lies in precisely this intermediate regime: we need enough batching to
obtain high aggregate throughput while preserving at least 50 output tokens/s/user. Furthermore, a
mixed-precision checkpoint may contain linear layers with different quantization schemes, whose
GEMM shapes and best backends differ. A single process-wide backend selection therefore couples
decisions that should be tuned independently.
The global linear backend remains the default, while a kernel configuration can override it for a
specific quantization scheme - for example, selecting a specialized backend for NVFP4 W4A16 layers
while leaving other linear layers on the platform default. This finer control lets a deployment choose
the best-supported GEMM implementation for each quantization scheme and accelerator without
maintaining separate model artifacts or forcing every quantized layer through the same path. It also

   • Reduces compute time by shifting work to Tensor Cores.
   • Reduces quantization error, by quantizing less often.
   • Enables larger batch sizes and speculation lengths.

5.5. Reducing the cost of Mamba checkpoints

Internal prefill checkpoints. In aligned Mamba cache mode, a reusable recurrent state must be
materialized at a cache-block boundary. The scheduling path can enforce this by ending a prefill
step at that boundary and issuing an additional model forward for the remainder of the prompt.
Although this produces the required state, the split repeats fixed forward-pass overhead and adds
another traversal of the Attention, MoE, routing, and tensor-parallel communication paths.
Internal prefill checkpointing was initially implemented in vLLM for Kimi K3. Building on that
work, we adapted the shared checkpoint builder and exporter to Mamba-2 in PR #57329.24 The
Mamba scan already materializes intermediate recurrent states at logical chunk boundaries. The
adapted path arranges for a logical chunk to end at the desired cache boundary, extracts the state
and convolution window there, and writes that checkpoint into the paged cache while the full prompt
remains in one model forward. A prompt that previously required an additional forward after the
checkpoint can therefore be processed in a single forward without sacrificing the reusable Mamba
state.
Taken together, these changes target the regime that matters most for Nemotron 3.5 Super deployment:
shared and growing contexts, interactive decode rates, and hardware-specific configurations chosen
from the useful portion of the throughput-latency frontier. Prefix-aware speculative decoding reduces
both repeated prefill and decode work, per-quantization backend selection exposes the kernel choices
needed to tune balanced operating points, and internal Mamba checkpoints reduce the cost of
creating the reusable state on which the workload depends.

In the low-concurrency regime targeted here, verifying a block of draft tokens costs similarly to
generating a single token, so the speedup is determined jointly by the acceptance length and the
latency of producing the draft. The recursive MTP head drafts one token per pass, so its drafting
latency grows with the draft length, while parallel drafters propose all draft tokens in a single forward
pass whose latency is nearly independent of the draft length. MTP is part of the model checkpoint
and requires no additional weights, whereas DFlash, DSpark, and LiLiCorr are separate drafter
checkpoints aimed at this regime.
A parallel drafter is trained on the marginal distribution at each block position, henceforth a slot,
so tokens selected independently at each slot can be individually plausible yet jointly incoherent.
DSpark addresses this with a semi-autoregressive design. It keeps the drafter body parallel and adds
a lightweight sequential head that biases the logits at each slot using a low-rank transition from the
token drafted at the previous slot. In the full DSpark design, a separate confidence head estimates
the probability that each draft prefix will be accepted, allowing the serving engine to adapt the
verification length to the request and system load. The Lightning 3.5 release used the lightweight
Markov correction but omitted this confidence head. LiLiCorr instead uses a lightweight, single-pass
correlation head to score compatibility across slots.

B. Ablation study of post-training quantization

B.1. Runaway loop: the termination failure in truncated rollouts

Reasoning length is not a defect; long reasoning in an agentic workload is mostly probing such
as running code, invoking tools, and reading back the result. A model’s abstention can fail by
two mechanisms: knowledge collapse, which produces hallucination, and reasoning collapse, which
produces termination failure, seen as semantic runaway looping behavior. Qualitatively, the runaway
loop is recognizable as failed negative proof : the model cannot certify a negative result from exhaustive
investigation, but confidently reopens a settled conclusion and cycles. Therefore, a non-terminating
agent emits more calls of ordinary length, with per-call statistics steady, rather than longer calls.
Eventually, the rollout’s last reasoning starts exploding in length, or the truncation rate increases.
We identify the runaway loop by reading reasoning passages in postmortem, while tracking the
failure of a model to terminate as it runs to the context wall. Table 31 reports the two immediately
measurable quantities: truncation, the fraction of rollouts containing at least one model call hitting
an output limit, and output length per rollout. The combination of these two quantities is a
useful early-warning indicator. For example, tau3-banking has 20.9% truncation and 35,173 output
tokens per rollout before MOPD, where we detect 19.7% runaway loop failures (153/776 rollouts).
After MOPD, truncation decreases to 0.3% with 13,669 output tokens per rollout, where only one

B.3. The damage of long-context retrieval on attention projections

     • Aggregation: there is no single target; evidence must be combined across many candidates.

B.4. Mamba output projection: FP4 weights, and the sensitivity is on generation length

The Mamba output projection differs from the expert fc2 in the input shape of the nonlinearity.
Expert fc2 is fed by a squared-ReLU activation, where 90–99 % of its input elements are exactly
zero before quantization, and only 1.05–3.32 values survive per FP4 block. Mamba SSM cache passes
a normalized SiLU gate to the output projection, which provides near-zero values but never exactly
zero. Consequently, the input of Mamba output projection carries near 16 survivors per FP4 block,
and 27.6% of elements fall below the amaxblock /24 rounding limit under FP4 activation quantization.
This tensor analysis stops us from taking the Mamba output projection to W4A4. Applying rotation
or higher activation precision can mitigate the flush.
We evaluated the impact of FP4 weight quantization with W4A16 against FP8 W8A8. Due to high
precision on activation, this contrast reveals the weight quantization effect directly. Table 38 shows
the output verbosity inflation caused by FP4 weight quantization. All six benchmarks move in
the same direction, with OSWorld and SciCode carrying the clearest inflation at 1.27× and 1.16×.
Note that each task accuracy is unchanged across two recipes. To address this issue, we preferred
parameter alignment as described in §4.4.

B.6. A precision recommendation with limitations

The same quantization recipe, which aggravated the termination failure before RL post-training,
reduced it afterwards. However, it does not modify model’s inherent structural effect. Across three
checkpoints spanning pre-RL and post-RL training, the stop-versus-continue geometry is invariant to
within 1 % (cosine 0.997, margin 0.996, noise-to-margin 1.001), while termination behavior changed
significantly. Together with the contribution profile, which is invariant to within 0.007 in routed
experts, this gives a clean statement: RL post-training did not move the lm_head or the
expert contribution structure; it changed where the hidden state points. A precision
recommendation can therefore be stated in structural terms, but its margin protection must be
checkpoint-dependent. Here are limitations of this study:
  1. The weight-versus-activation attribution for the termination failure is an inference from a
     bound: we did not experiment with W16A4.
  2. Expert GEMM output error and benchmark verbosity are two separate measurements, consis-
     tent in sign and magnitude. Nothing traces a specific displacement to a specific extra token;
     the relationship is coherent, not demonstrated causal.
  3. The attention score-path dilution is measured on clean weights as structure, not as a perturbation
     response.
  4. Every configuration in this ablation study, including the BF16 reference, serves an FP8 KV
     cache; the ladder dilution is therefore present in the baseline too, and the projection deltas are
     measured on top of an already-diluted score.
  5. The MRCR-128k’s disambiguation loss is measured against an unquantized reference with an
     FP8 KV cache, but quantization uses a full configuration: FP8 KV cache, FP4 routed experts
     and FP4 Mamba output projection. The KV cache is identified as the damage carrier on
     Nemotron 3.5 Lightning study, but Nemotron 3.5 Super VL ablation does not address BF16
     KV precision to contrast its damage.
  6. MRCR-128k is the only disambiguation instrument in the suite, so that limb of the result
     rests on one benchmark. It carries three repetitions per task, which is enough to measure the
     decoding-noise floor but not to support per-stratum claims beyond the reported candidate-count
     split.
  7. The unembedding measurements use a proxy input, norm𝑓 (hidden + residual) at the last layer,
     i.e., the true input minus that layer’s own MoE contribution. Both tests receive the identical
     input, so the comparison isolates unembedding quantization exactly; the absolute rates are not
     the model’s true output on those tokens.
  8. The contribution analysis uses two fixed token windows, one per training stage (pre-MOPD
     versus MOPD). Within a stage the BF16-versus-FP4 contrast holds the fixed window to isolate
     the weights, which establishes the invariant contribution profile with or without quantization.
     The cross-stage comparison changes both the weights and the window, so it loosely highlights
     a structural change but does not pinpoint the exact root cause.

ones, have no matches.

the sample itself contains code, markup, or instructions. The prompt asks for the reasoning before
the verdict, and for a severity that expresses how important removal is, separately from the label.

C.4. Synthetic Evaluation

Ground-truth contamination labels do not exist for real data, so we evaluate the pipeline on synthetic
data. We take all questions of a target benchmark and transform each with 13 augmentations, at
four strengths (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9) that scale the intensity of the change. The augmentations are:
   • surface: verbatim copy, character noise, case flipping, whitespace insertion
   • structural: cropping to the beginning, middle, or end, word dropout, local word reordering,
     burial among other questions
   • semantic, generated by another LLM: paraphrase (from light rewording to a full restyle),
     translation (into increasingly distant languages), and abstraction (progressive generalization of
     the problem).
Every transformed question is a positive by construction. As negatives we add unmodified questions
from a different benchmark in the same domain, chosen to be hard to tell apart from the target:
SWE-bench Pro for Terminal-Bench 2.1 (4,361 positives, 731 negatives) and MMLU-Pro for GPQA
Diamond (9,702 positives, 12,032 negatives). The LLM augmentations are generated with a model
different from the judge, so that the judge cannot recognize its own rewrites.
Table 42 reports each signal applied alone, at thresholds that flag no negatives. The judge recovers
most positives with a single false positive in 12,763 negatives. Verbatim overlap recovers about a
quarter (essentially the copies and mild surface edits), and semantic overlap about half. Recall is lower
on GPQA, whose short multiple-choice questions carry less distinctive text than Terminal-Bench’s
long task descriptions.
Table 43 breaks recall down by augmentation, averaged over strengths. Copies, case changes,
reordering, and burial in longer text are recovered almost completely, as are paraphrases. Translations
are recovered almost entirely through the judge. The misses concentrate in aggressive crops, heavy
word dropout, and strong abstraction at the highest strengths, where the transformed text often no
longer identifies the source question. Because every transformed question is counted as a positive
regardless of how far it drifted, these recall values are conservative.

C.5. Notable Cases

The removed samples illustrate why contamination cannot be reduced to string matching. We
describe a few patterns found in the judge’s decisions without reproducing the benchmark items.
  • Translated and reformulated problems. Several MATH-500 problems appear in the training
    data translated into Chinese, some also reformulated to ask for a quantity derived from the
    original answer (e.g. the sum of the numerator and denominator of a fraction). Such samples
    share almost no n-grams with the benchmark, yet still teach the answer.
  • Cross-lingual variants of chat prompts. Some benchmark prompts reappear in RLHF prompt
    collections in other languages (Chinese, Japanese, Spanish, Polish, Russian, German, French),
    sometimes with changed numbers, as in a widely shared short-distance travel puzzle posed
    with 50 instead of 500 meters.
  • Benchmark tasks inside agentic data. Terminal-Bench task descriptions appear nearly verbatim
    inside agentic RL prompts, prefixed with environment-setup instructions and with different file
    paths.
  • Expert-level questions. Questions from Humanity’s Last Exam appear in math and tool-
    integrated reasoning data as copies, paraphrases, or problems whose solution contains the
    benchmark answer as an intermediate step.
All those cases were prevented from entering the training mix.

C.6. Limitations

  • Recall is bounded by candidate selection. The judge only rules on pairs selected by the cheap
    signals. A contaminated sample that is not among a question’s top candidates, or that falls
    below the similarity gate, is always kept.
  • Only the problem statement is scored. Contamination that stem from using the same set of
    answers or other artifacts is not detected.
  • The evaluation is a proxy. The augmentations approximate, but do not exhaust, the ways
    contamination arises in practice.
