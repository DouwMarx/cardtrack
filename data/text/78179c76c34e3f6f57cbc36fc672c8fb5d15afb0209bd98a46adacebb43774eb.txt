1. Introduction
Executive Summary
This report describes the results of safety, security, and alignment testing conducted on
Gemini 4 Carbon, the next model in our Gemini 4 series. Aligned with our Frontier Safety
Framework and Google’s AI Principles, we run rigorous safety and security evaluations
designed to ensure the model is safe, helpful, and resilient against misuse. For basic
model information, see the Gemini 4 Carbon Model Card.

Unless otherwise noted, evaluations in this report benchmark the Carbon model without
out-of-model production safeguards and without search grounding to measure intrinsic
model behavior, comparing performance against recently released Gemini models
(Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8 Flash). In production deployments, additional safeguards
are applied at inference-time to provide multiple layers of protections, as described in
Section 2.

Key takeaways about Gemini 4 Carbon’s safety and security performance are below.

Mitigating Frontier Safety Risks: Our Frontier Safety Framework comprises a set of
protocols that aims to address severe risks that may arise from the high-impact
capabilities of frontier models. Under our Material Capability Change (MCC) assessment,
Gemini 4 Carbon did not show meaningful new capabilities or material increases in
performance compared with Gemini 4 Argon. For this reason, a full critical capability
assessment was not required under the FSF, and we carry forward our capability
evaluation results and threshold determinations from Gemini 4 Argon: we cannot rule out
that the model without out-of-model mitigations reaches the Cyber Uplift Level 1 Critical
Capability Level (CCL), the CBRN Uplift Level 1 CCL for biological information risks, or the
Stealth and Situational Awareness Tracked Capability Level (TCL), while we rule out the
CBRN TCL for chemical, radiological, and nuclear domains as well as the Harmful
Manipulation and ML R&D CCLs. As with Gemini 4 Argon, we have deployed appropriate
mitigations, and assess that the residual risk has been lowered to an acceptable level
across all of these domains. In particular, given the increases in cyber exploitation
benchmark scores, we focused on hardening our cyber misuse protections compared to
Argon to ensure mitigation robustness remains proportional to the model’s risk. In our
testing, Carbon also shows further improvements in alignment propensities compared to
Argon. See Section 3 for full results.

Preventing Content Safety & Agentic Misuse: Being resilient to attempts to elicit
dangerous or other types of prohibited content from the model or to misuse the model as

an agent. Across our prohibited content evaluations, Gemini 4 Carbon improves upon
Gemini 4 Argon. In agent harnesses (tested in Antigravity), Gemini 4 Carbon achieves a
higher safe action rate than both Argon and 3.8 Flash across malicious tool-use requests
covering prohibited actions, physical harm, and scaled abuse, while performing
comparably to both models on helpfulness for benign requests. We have also
substantially expanded our real-time and offline anti-distillation detection and
enforcement stack, and addressed avenues for unauthorized extraction of reasoning
traces associated with thought signatures. See Section 4 for more information.

Promoting Safe and Aligned Model Interactions: Supporting good-faith model
interactions that promote user wellbeing and stay aligned with user intent. On consumer
health evaluations, Carbon improves over Argon and 3.8 Flash across static text,
document, and image understanding, and dynamic multi-turn conversations requiring
proactive context-seeking. In adversarial mental health evaluations, Carbon shows further
improvements over both Argon and 3.8 Flash. On agentic alignment evaluations, Carbon
reduces both overall under-service and overreach relative to Argon: it further improves
infeasibility recognition over Argon, pauses to clarify with the user more reliably on
ambiguous or high-risk tasks, and passes most tasks on our agentic honesty benchmark -
disclosing planted experimental or methodological problems to the user rather than
asserting unsupported claims. Carbon performs comparably to Argon on tone quality and
neutrality. On hallucination evaluations, Carbon achieves higher composite
accuracy-and-coverage scores on closed-book benchmarks and further improves
faithfulness to source documents. See Section 5 for details.

Protecting users from indirect prompt injection attacks: Being robust to prompt
injection attacks and other attempts by malicious actors to bypass security measures and
harm model users. Gemini 4 Carbon demonstrates indirect prompt injection (IPI)
robustness similar to or exceeding Gemini 4 Argon across our evaluation suites, and far
ahead of Gemini 3.8 Flash. On Gray Swan’s external Indirect Prompt Injection benchmark
(where Gemini 4 Argon previously achieved the lowest attack success rate of 0.7% among
tested frontier models), Gemini 4 Carbon resisted all attacks to achieve a 0% attack
success rate. At the same time, we know that prompt injection robustness remains an
area for continued improvement and prompt injection attacks on Carbon are still
possible. See Section 6 for details.

Strengthening Cyber Defense Capabilities: We have trained Gemini 4 Carbon to
empower defenders, namely to accelerate security teams to find, validate and remediate
vulnerabilities at scale while mitigating dual-use risks. In comprehensive evaluations
across public and internal benchmarks, Gemini 4 Carbon advances performance in
autonomous code remediation. On CWE-bench v1, an end-to-end benchmark testing

autonomous security audits and patch synthesis across 120 tasks (73 CWEs) and six
programming languages without prior bug localization, Gemini 4 Carbon achieved a
71.0% Pass@1 rate, up from 68.0% with Gemini 4 Argon. Crucially, the model’s patches
resolve root-cause vulnerabilities while cleanly preserving existing functional and
regression test suites, establishing a strong foundation for scaling automated
vulnerability patching and defensive tooling like CodeMender.

We also evaluate Gemini 4 Carbon’s capability across vulnerability discovery and exploit
generation, which is increasingly crucial to validate the criticality of vulnerabilities found:
ExploitBench, ExploitGym, SEC-bench Pro, and internal red-teaming benchmarks based
on real-world infrastructure tests. Carbon reached a 54.8% success rate (causal pass rate)
on ExploitGym (a 12.8 percentage point improvement over Gemini 4 Argon). Our safety
framework balances capability with robust governance, trusted access programs for
verified defenders such as the Fairwind Program, and runtime intervention. Our policies
prohibit weaponized exploit generation through additional safety layers such as
out-of-model input/output classifiers, activation-based introspection probes, and
monitoring frameworks that supervise agent trajectories and preserve chain of thought
transparency. See Section 2 and Section 7 for more information.

2. Safety and Security Mitigations
This section outlines the safety and security mitigations for Gemini models and deployments.
We take a defense-in-depth approach, layering independent safeguards at the model, system
and account levels so that if one fails, others are designed to catch issues before any user is
impacted.

2.1 Model Training
We build safety into our models through a combination of pre-training and post-training
approaches. We start by constructing metrics based on the established safety policies and
desired behaviors, which we typically turn into automated evaluations that guide model
development through successive model iterations. This pipeline relies on data filtering,
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), and Reinforcement Learning (RL). Below, we explain these
approaches:

   ●​ Dataset filtering: We apply safety filtering to our pre-training data for our strictest
      policies, including for child sexual abuse and exploitation material, personally identifiable
      information and content from low-trust URLs. We also filter out low-quality, repeated
      patterns from AI-generated content on the web.
   ●​ Pre-training monitoring: Starting with Gemini 2.0, we developed a novel evaluation to
      capture the model’s ability to be steered towards different viewpoints and values, which
      helps align the model at post-training time.
   ●​ Post-training data: We source adversarial prompts by either leveraging existing
      models and tools to probe Gemini’s attack surface, or relying on human interactions to
      discover potentially harmful behavior. Throughout this process, we strive for coverage
      of our safety policies across common model use cases and realistic adversarial attack
      patterns. We specifically emphasize scenarios where our models need improvement on
      safety/neutrality violations, over-refusals, or tone. These prompts are subsequently
      added to our post-training mixtures in SFT and/or RL, as described below.
   ●​ Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): For the SFT stage, we use a combination of synthetic
      sources and human steering to produce high-quality targets for training. Throughout
      this phase, we heavily rely on automated filtering to ensure our demonstrations meet
      various policy and quality standards. The process described here is typically refined
      through successive model iterations, and we use automated evaluations on both safety
      and non-safety metrics to monitor impact and potential unintended regressions.
   ●​ Reinforcement Learning (RL): The reward signal during RL comes from various
      sources, including a reward model trained on human preferences and rubric-guided

                                                                                                  7
       LLM judge feedback. These rewards similarly cover various aspects of our
       safety/neutrality policies, including policy violations, over-refusals, and tone. During
       model development, we also rely on a number of evaluations and analyses, both offline
       and online, to monitor for safety and other regressions.

2.2 Inference-time Safeguards and Interventions
Out-of-model safeguards serve as an independent layer of protection deployed outside the
model. When implemented, this layer leverages high-recall and high-precision input, output,
and thought-based classifiers to evaluate text and multimodal content in real time.

To promote safety across various deployment surfaces, the following inference-time
safeguards may be used as appropriate:

   ●​ Prompted Classifiers: These LLM-based classifiers take in user prompts, model
      responses, or conversations and detect whether the content appears malicious or risky.
   ●​ Fine-tuned Prompted Classifier: This classifier is fine-tuned on the full weights of a
      small Gemini model to detect malicious or risky user requests or model responses.
   ●​ Introspection Probes: These classifiers examine the internal hidden activations of the
      main model to detect latent representations of requests and responses for harmful
      content. Developed with a diverse, high-volume synthetic dataset, they provide a fast
      and highly robust solution that is difficult for black-box attackers to evade.
   ●​ Regex, YARA Rules and Embedding Classifiers: These traditional classifiers use
       straightforward pattern-matching to achieve near-instant reaction times for newly
       escalated threats and provide additional signals for other layers.
   ●​ Chain of Thought (CoT) Monitors: These analyze the model's internal reasoning
      process to detect and, when appropriate, block harmful intent in complex or adversarial
      queries before a final response is generated.

Inference-time interventions are designed to prevent violative model responses while
preserving benign utility. Depending on the deployment surface, either a "system instruction"
(safe-completion) or "hard punting" (direct refusal) is applied based on the classifier labels.

If the model is able to fulfill the user intent without violating our content policies, system
instructions guide the model to provide a safe, helpful response that excludes any actionable
components that could lead to harm. We consider a response to be violative if it helps with
attacks in a concrete, actionable way. We consider a response to be non-violative if it is
abstract, generic, or consists of information that is publicly available, or easily accessible via
resources such as textbooks, articles, etc. For example, in the cyber domain, the model will

                                                                                                     8
fulfill requests such as analyzing known public exploits or discussing defensive coding
practices, but will hard-punt on requests to write functional, weaponized exploits.

2.3 Post-Launch Monitoring and Account Enforcement
We monitor for intentional misuse of our models, especially for frontier safety risks. Where
necessary and appropriate, we carry out account-level enforcement actions to limit or disable
users’ access to Gemini and other Google products. We also use these monitoring signals to
identify areas for online mitigation improvement.

To support these efforts, we use a variety of signals to identify relevant logs, which then pass
through multiple levels of LLM-based classifiers to surface users attempting to elicit dangerous
content. The highest scoring logs flagged by this system go through a robust review process to
determine true positive malicious intent. This process includes examination of past
conversations. Violations can lead to a range of enforcement actions against offending user
accounts, including blocking Gemini access or account disablement. Google uses a variety of
methods to prevent these users from creating new accounts.

2.4 AI Control
The Google DeepMind AI Control Roadmap treats these controls as a second line of defense
behind model alignment, limiting the harm a misaligned model could cause through
system-level mitigations such as monitoring, prevention, and access controls. The roadmap
introduces TRAIT&R, a taxonomy of the tactics and techniques an agentic adversary could use
to cause loss of control, sabotage, or direct harm, which we use to guide monitoring coverage.
Mitigations follow two capability-mitigation ladders, one for detection and one for prevention
and response. These ladders escalate our safeguards as models become increasingly capable
of evading oversight and carrying out attacks.

2.4.1 Monitoring and Prevention

We implement these mitigations through two complementary approaches. First, we ensure
that our evaluation and training environments are safe and secure through proactive scanning
with highly capable cybersecurity models (e.g., Gemini 4 Carbon). This hardening process finds
and remediates novel vulnerabilities that might permit the model to inappropriately access
external resources, including the Internet.

Second, we leverage our agent monitoring and response platform to monitor model activity.
This platform combines deterministic policy checks with chain of thought and action monitors
that run on trusted models. When the platform flags an action, it is designed to proactively

                                                                                                9
mitigate anomalous behavior, or send an alert for automated triage and investigation. High
impact alerts are handled by a dedicated incident response team. We used this monitoring
platform throughout Gemini's development to monitor tens of millions of agent trajectories
during training, evaluations, and when used for internal tasks.

Chain of thought monitoring is one of our most effective mechanisms for detecting
misalignment. While signals from our monitors serve as a valuable flywheel for our hardening
efforts, we are careful with their use elsewhere in the training pipeline. In particular, to preserve
our chain of thought monitoring capability, we avoid training directly against our monitors, or
using monitor outputs as a training signal or intervention. We strongly encourage the rest of
the industry to preserve reasoning transparency so that model reasoning remains useful for
identifying misalignment.

2.5 Trusted Access
As models reach new capability thresholds in high-stakes domains, we recognize their
dual-use potential. It is also imperative that we introduce these capabilities safely and securely.
To manage this, we provide access to our most advanced models through trusted access
programs, ensuring these powerful capabilities remain exclusively in the hands of verified
defenders, trusted research partners, and government entities.

2.5.1 Fairwind Program

Through the Fairwind Program we provide verified defenders, government partners and
security researchers with advanced cyber capabilities to build better defenses. In the Fairwind
program, misuse mitigations are tailored to permit authorized users to perform a range of
specialized cybersecurity tasks—such as validating the exploitability of a vulnerability, threat
simulation, reverse engineering, and comprehensive malware analysis—safely and efficiently.

To expand access to dual-use tasks to more defenders, we continuously monitor model
activity, leveraging similar agent monitoring and response platforms as for broader AI control.
This approach preserves essential cyber defense utility and empowers the research
community to proactively find and remediate novel vulnerabilities, while continuing to monitor
for misalignment. Further details regarding the structure of the program and partnership
requirements are available in our Fairwind Program announcement.

3. Frontier Safety
3.1 Frontier Safety Overview
We released our Frontier Safety Framework (FSF) in May 2024 (v1) and updated it in February
2025 (v2), September 2025 (v3) and April 2026 (v3.1). The FSF comprises a set of protocols that
aims to address severe risks that may arise from the high-impact capabilities of frontier
models. It complements Google’s suite of AI responsibility and safety practices, and enables
Google’s AI innovation and deployment consistent with our AI Principles.

As part of our risk identification exercise outlined in the FSF, we have identified four risk
domains where, based on early research, we have determined severe risk may be most likely to
arise from future models: CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear information
risks), cybersecurity, harmful manipulation, and loss of control. In identifying these domains we
followed the structured process in Section 1.3.1 of the FSF: we compiled candidate risks,
drawing on model-independent information (literature, incident databases, threat intelligence),
post-market monitoring and incident information from the Gemini 3 series, and information
shared by governments and AI safety institutes; we analyzed the nature and sources of each
candidate risk; and we prioritized those with a plausible pathway to severe harm at scale.

Google monitors risks across a range of domains. Candidate risks considered but not
prioritized as separate domains under the FSF (e.g. risks to public health, safety, and public
security) are addressed through content safety policies and general safeguards and remain
under review. The evaluations and assessment of these risks are set out in the rest of the
Gemini 4 Carbon Safety and Security Report. As part of our broader research into and
development of frontier models, we continue to assess whether there are other domains
where significant or severe risks may arise and will update our approach as appropriate.

Our FSF is built around capability thresholds called “Critical Capability Levels” (CCLs). These are
capability levels at which, absent mitigation measures, frontier models or systems may pose
heightened risk of severe harm. CCLs are determined by identifying and analyzing the main
foreseeable paths through which a model could cause severe harm: we then define the CCLs
as the minimal set of capabilities a model must possess to do so. In v3.1 of the Framework, we
introduced “Tracked Capability Levels (TCLs).” TCLs are meant to capture significant risks that
may manifest at a lower capability threshold than our CCLs. We identify TCLs for CBRN risk, as
well as ML R&D and misalignment risks.

3.1.1 Lack of Material Capability Change and Scope of FSF Assessment

As outlined in our FSF, we conduct a critical capability assessment prior to the first external
deployment of a new frontier model. For external deployment of subsequent versions of the
model, we determine whether a substantial modification has been made and whether a further
critical capability assessment is required if (1) the model has meaningful new capabilities or
material increases in performance; and (2) we believe such a material capability increase could
undermine the justification for why the risks stemming from the model are acceptable.

To understand if a subsequent version of the model has meaningful new capabilities or material
increases in performance relative to the most recent model version subject to a critical
capability assessment, we conduct a Material Capability Change (MCC) assessment. We do
this by measuring its general capability using GCI v2, our internal General Capability Index
modeled on the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI).1 Like the ECI, GCI v2 combines a model’s
performance across many diverse benchmarks into a single overall capability score. We treat a
difference in GCI scores as material if it exceeds four months of capability progress, estimated
from a line of best fit over frontier models. Across historical frontier Gemini releases, this
general capability score has increased at a rate of roughly 4.7 GCI points per month.

Carrying forward these determinations means that the high inherent capability levels we
identified in Gemini 4 Argon continue to apply to Gemini 4 Carbon. In particular, in our Argon

Notably, even though Gemini 4 Carbon did not demonstrate a significant capability
improvement according to our Material Capability Change assessment, we did observe a
strong improvement over Argon’s already high baseline in cybersecurity on ExploitBench and
ExploitGym (see Section 7.1.1). As a result, we commensurately improved our suite of
mitigations. Our assessment for Gemini 4 Carbon focuses on ensuring the assumptions of our

safety arguments still hold by verifying both the effectiveness of our mitigations and the

Further, even when a new model iteration does not demonstrate meaningful new capabilities
on our MCC assessment, changes in post-training or helpfulness could still make it easier for
adversaries to bypass or overcome safeguards, shift the model’s baseline refusal propensities,
or reduce the legibility of the CoT reasoning that our oversight mechanisms rely on. For Gemini
4 Carbon, we therefore focused our testing on:

   ●​ CBRN propensity assessments: We ran static propensity evaluations on the model
      without out-of-model safeguards to verify its baseline refusal behavior on CBRN
      threats.
   ●​ Misuse safeguards assessments: We evaluated the coverage and adversarial
      robustness of our deployed CBRN and cybersecurity safeguards, including through
      internal jailbreak evaluations and external red-teaming with Gray Swan and Trajectory
      Labs.
   ●​ Alignment assessments: We ran a condensed version of our alignment evaluations to
      verify that model updates did not introduce new concerning misalignment behaviors.
   ●​ CoT transparency assessments: We audited our training procedure, ran a targeted
      check confirming that single-pass reasoning without CoT remained unchanged (see
      Section 3.4.3.1), and evaluated the legibility, coverage, and monitorability of the model’s
      reasoning traces to confirm that our CoT-based oversight mechanisms remain reliable.

3.3 Misuse Safeguards
Gemini models are protected by layered safeguards that span the model development and
deployment lifecycle. By default, all models include guardrails covering a broad range of
harmful behaviors, with dedicated, higher-assurance mitigations targeting domains near or at
our Tracked and Critical Capability Levels (T/CCLs). Because frontier capabilities are most
advanced in cybersecurity and CBRN — and because we carry forward our capability threshold
determinations from Gemini 4 Argon while observing a modest capability gain on offensive
cybersecurity benchmarks (Section 3.1.1) — we deploy Gemini 4 Carbon with an updated suite
of input/output classifiers, activation-based introspection probes, and agentic monitoring rules
targeting these domains. Our safeguards and approach to safeguards may change over time
as we gain experience and insights on the projected capabilities of future models, changes in
mitigation methodologies and effectiveness, as well as the evolving threat landscapes.

We evaluate the strength of our model training and deployment guardrails along two primary
dimensions:

   ●​ Coverage: Assuming threat actors don’t try to circumvent these safeguards, how much
      assistance would they get from the model based on what our mitigations are/aren’t
      blocking?
   ●​ Adversarial Robustness: If threat actors do try to circumvent safeguards on “covered”
      topics, how well would they be able to do so?

This section reports the performance of Gemini 4 Carbon's deployed safeguards across both
dimensions relative to the strong baselines established by Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8
Flash.

3.3.1 Coverage Evaluations
Our coverage evaluations measure safeguard performance across both malicious and benign
cybersecurity and CBRN workflows. Grounded in domain-specific threat models and policy
redlines, these datasets combine subject-matter-expert authoring, curated dual-use stress
tests, and sanitized traffic distributions vetted by human raters.

We track two primary metrics across coverage benchmarks:

   ●​ Violation Rate: The proportion of malicious queries or tasks on which the safeguarded
      system provides actionable, policy-violating assistance (lower is better).
   ●​ Benign Refusal Rate: The proportion of benign, dual-use, or defensive queries that the
      system unnecessarily refuses (lower is better).

We evaluate CBRN safeguard coverage across conversational threat scenarios, agentic

3.3.2 Adversarial Robustness

Even when safeguards achieve near-complete coverage on direct requests, determined threat
actors attempt to circumvent them using jailbreaks encompassing adversarial prompt
wrappers, multi-turn conversational steering, automated red-teaming orchestrators, and
stateful agentic tool-use harnesses.

                                                                                              23
3.3.2.1 How We Evaluate Jailbreak Severity & Robustness

Not all safeguard bypasses pose equal real-world risk. Drawing on Anthropic’s jailbreak severity
framework, we evaluate adversarial findings across four dimensions:

Capability Gain (Uplift): How far the elicited output advances an attacker beyond publicly
available tools, search engines, or standard documentation (ranging from 0 for
textbook/attacker-supplied concepts to high uplift for actionable, end-to-end exploit or
weaponization material).

Breadth of Capability Gain (Universality): Whether a technique is universal—unlocking
harmful outputs across diverse tasks and threat categories—or narrow, succeeding only on a
single target or vulnerability after bespoke tuning.

Ease of Weaponization (Operational Friction): How much human expertise, compute, and
retry effort is required to turn a technique into a working attack—distinguishing turnkey
jailbreaks (single drop-in prompts that succeed on the first try) from high-friction attacks
requiring hours of expert steering or multi-context decomposition across dozens of turns.

Discoverability: How easily a threat actor can discover or replicate the bypass.

We test the adversarial robustness of our safeguards in three ways: (1) static and dynamic
automated jailbreak attacks applied to high-severity cyber and biological seed objectives; (2)
stateful, multi-attempt automated red-teaming agents operating inside live sandboxed cyber
environments; and (3) independent automated and manual red-teaming conducted with
external partners, including Gray Swan and Trajectory Labs.

Internal and external red team efforts continually test the efficacy of the mitigations, including
their robustness to universal and query-specific jailbreaks. Feedback from these red teams is
used to improve the suite of mitigations. We continue to invest in automated and manual red
teaming to improve mitigation jailbreak robustness and coverage.

In our quantitative benchmarks, we capture the distinction between turnkey reliability and
eventual persistence by reporting two complementary metrics for each evaluation:

   ●​ Query Success Rate (QSR): The percentage of individual adversarial prompts or
      queries sent to the model that result in an actionable policy breach. Low QSR indicates
      that drop-in or turnkey attacks fail consistently.
   ●​ Objective Success Rate (OSR): The percentage of distinct underlying harmful
      objectives (seed prompts) for which the attacker achieves at least one actionable
      breach across all attempted templates, turns, or retries. Comparing OSR against QSR

                                                                                                 24
       measures how much retry volume and operational friction an attacker must expend to
       fulfill a single goal.

3.3.3 Conclusion

Our evaluations show that Gemini 4 Carbon’s safeguards provide substantial coverage,
successfully blocking the majority of actionable queries within CBRN and cyber attack chains.
Note that rather than completely eliminating the possibility of harmful outputs, the primary
function of these safeguards is to impose significant operational friction: requiring threat
actors to expend added labor, time, and compute to successfully engage in misuse. While this
friction meaningfully reduces capability uplift, it may nonetheless be possible for sophisticated
or persistent actors to achieve partial bypasses given sufficient resource expenditure (though
our threat modeling suggests this is unlikely). This is why our misuse prevention approach
incorporates additional layers of defense such as log monitoring and account enforcement.

To maintain the relevance of our evaluations against evolving attacker behavior, we monitor for
novel jailbreaks via internal red teaming, automated discovery pipelines, and external threat
intelligence. Jailbreaks that we assess as particularly risky are escalated for mitigation (e.g.,
blocklists, filter updates, or model fine-tuning), and the most novel, effective strategies are
used to expand and update our robustness evaluation datasets. Although this pipeline is
inherently reactive and subject to a discovery lag, this feedback loop aims to reduce the
effective lifespan of jailbreaks.

3.4.1 Holistic Assessment of Loss of Control Risks
We assess that deploying Gemini 4 Carbon does not materially increase loss of control risk
relative to our Gemini 4 Argon Safety and Security Report (Section 3.4.3), with residual risk
remaining below the Stealth and Situational Awareness Tracked Capability Level (TCL). In that
assessment, we found it unlikely that Argon would cause material real-world harm by
autonomously evading oversight and controls, and highly unlikely that it would cause severe
harm or open-ended escape – based on hardened sandboxes, code-review gates,
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and action monitoring, incident response, and evidence that observed
misbehavior was aimed at local task completion rather than beyond-episode goals. As in that
report, these findings depend on the continued validity of the key assumptions set out in
Section 3.4.3.4 of the Argon report. Because Gemini 4 Carbon did not show meaningful new
capabilities or material increases in performance (Section 3.1.1), we did not repeat that holistic
assessment in full, and instead examine here whether changes in Carbon's capabilities or
propensities alter its conclusions.

On capabilities, we carry forward our threshold determinations from Gemini 4 Argon (Section
3.1.1), and baseline per-turn reasoning without CoT is statistically unchanged (Section 3.4.3.1).
Two changes nonetheless bear on this assessment. First, Carbon's stronger performance on
offensive cybersecurity benchmarks (Section 3.1.1) does not overturn the feasibility analysis in
our Gemini 4 Argon report (Section 3.4.3.3), which already assumed an unmitigated model
could compromise vulnerable systems and relied instead on sandboxing, code-review gates,
and misalignment monitoring; however, stronger cyber capability increases the importance of
those controls, misuse safeguards (Section 3.3), and low overreach propensity. Second, Gemini
4 Carbon more frequently recognizes when it is in a synthetic safety evaluation, making
dedicated safety-evaluation suites harder to interpret on their own; our alignment update
therefore relies more heavily on capability-benchmark transcripts and our analysis of RL and
internal deployment trajectories (Section 3.4.2).

On propensities, we refresh our Alignment Assessment (Section 3.4.2) and Chain-of-Thought
Transparency Assessment (Section 3.4.3) below. Compared with Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 4
Carbon exhibits fewer shortcut and cheating attempts on audited coding capability
benchmarks and triggers substantially fewer misalignment-monitor alerts across
reinforcement learning (RL) training and internal coding deployments. It also shows zero overt
beyond-episode goals or unprompted sabotage across audited trajectories, and maintains
near-ceiling CoT legibility and step coverage in normal operation across re-evaluated settings.

3.4.2.4 Overall takeaways and limitations

Overall, our refreshed evaluations remain consistent with both alignment dimensions in our
Gemini 4 Argon Safety and Security Report (Section 3.4.4). Compared with the low baselines
established by Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 4 Carbon makes fewer shortcut and cheating attempts
on audited coding capability benchmarks and triggers substantially fewer
misalignment-monitor alerts across RL training and internal coding deployments. Across
100,000 audited RL rollouts, low-friction instrumental-convergence scenarios, and 3,021
internal-codebase honeypot runs, we also observed zero overt alignment faking,
self-preservation, or unprompted sabotage.

Each source of evidence has distinct limitations, so our assessment relies on combining them.
Because Gemini 4 Carbon more frequently recognizes dedicated safety evaluations as tests,
synthetic safety suites are harder to interpret on their own. Auditing capability benchmarks
avoids planted safety traps, though near-zero cheating rates leave little statistical headroom
and make results sensitive to how ambiguous task prompts are interpreted and which runs our
initial screener escalates for full review. Comparing alerts across RL training and internal coding
deployments spans far more trajectories in realistic workflows, but reflects differences in
trajectory length, tool calls per task, task mixtures, and checkpoint selection alongside model
propensity. Finally, our RL rollout audit and sabotage evaluations examine single-task
trajectories and overt reasoning or actions, and cannot by themselves rule out covert
long-horizon goals concealed without verbalization.

3.4.3 Chain-of-Thought Transparency Assessment

3.4.3.1 Summary of Findings

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) monitoring is a central safeguard in our Loss of Control architecture,
relying on reasoning traces remaining legible and faithful on complex tasks (Section 3.4.5 of the
Gemini 4 Argon Safety and Security Report; Korbak et al., 2025). Across our refreshed
evaluations, we find no evidence of degraded CoT monitorability relative to Gemini 4 Argon.
Our audit of newly introduced RL training and reward configurations identified no new
pressures that we believe pose a meaningful threat to monitorability, and baseline reasoning
without CoT is statistically unchanged. In normal operation, CoT legibility and step coverage

                                                                                                 41
remain above 99% across evaluated settings, while unprompted reasoning about CoT
monitoring remains rare with zero observed deceptive thought adjustments.

Because Gemini 4 Carbon did not show meaningful new capabilities or material increases in
performance under our MCC assessment (Section 3.1.1), we carry forward the prompted
monitor-evasion, steganography, and CoT-controllability results from our Gemini 4 Argon
report. To sanity check that our material capability change assessment is also applicable for
these assessments, alongside our refreshed CoT propensity evaluations below we also ran a
targeted check with thinking disabled across the 19-task No Chain of Thought Reasoning Index
(NCRI; Nanda, 2026; Ho et al., 2025) and synthetic multi-hop serial depth. Gemini 4 Carbon was
statistically indistinguishable from Gemini 4 Argon both without filler tokens (150.1 vs 150.3 on
NCRI, where +10 points doubles above-chance problem-solving odds; 5.6 vs 5.5 steps at 50%
accuracy) and with filler tokens (153.0 vs 152.1; 5.75 vs 5.54 steps; Pfau et al., 2024; Greenblatt,
2026).

3.4.3.2 Model Training Procedure

Model training, indirect optimization pressures, or emerging capabilities could incentivize
models to obfuscate their reasoning or use steganography (Korbak et al., 2025). Given this, it is
important to understand and assess what impact our training procedures could have on the
faithfulness of CoT.

For Gemini 4 Carbon, we use both supervised fine-tuning on thought traces and reinforcement
learning (RL). For supervised fine-tuning, the thought traces are generated by reasoning
models, rather than being written manually. The RL stage includes length penalties on the
thoughts, to teach the model to reason efficiently. Beyond surface-level penalties of this kind,
RL rewards are intended to evaluate outputs rather than thought content; however, learned
components used to assign token-level credit for those output rewards observe thoughts as
context, applying content-dependent optimization pressure to individual thought tokens. Using
the framework of Kaufmann et al. (2026), wherein the strongest obfuscation risks come from
signals that conflict with output rewards, this credit-assignment objective is structurally aligned
with the output reward, though inductive biases or approximation errors in a learned
credit-assignment model could still exert direct optimization pressure on thought content.

We audited the reinforcement learning (RL) training and reward configurations newly
introduced since Argon for mechanisms that could pressure Gemini 4 Carbon's chain of
thought to become less legible or monitorable (noting that issues from prior reports can still
carry forward when training builds on earlier checkpoints). Within this scope, we identified no
new RL training or reward pressures that we believe pose a meaningful threat to monitorability.

                                                                                                 42
We found two minor cases in Gemini 4 Carbon's RL training where thoughts were not fully
stripped before reward scoring. First, when trajectories hit a maximum token-generation limit
before closing their thought block, unclosed thoughts were inadvertently passed to a few
general task and preference rewards, though never to rewards targeting safety or cheating.
Because cut-off trajectories almost always received zero or negative total reward regardless
(98.7% of the time) and leaked thoughts produced nonzero scores on under 0.002% of RL
rollouts, any resulting pressure on the chain of thought was negligible.

Second, during a small portion of Carbon's RL training, an auxiliary grading model inadvertently
received part of the model's thoughts before the bug was fixed. This affected only a very small
fraction of Carbon's overall RL training, and a matched control re-run with thoughts masked

security to protect our users, data, and intellectual property, including frontier models.
Google’s Secure AI Framework (SAIF) is a defense-in-depth approach that embeds security
into every layer of our AI systems—from data and infrastructure to models and applications.
SAIF is premised on six core elements, including strong security foundations, detection and
response, automated defenses, harmonized platform-level controls, adaptable controls to
mitigate emerging threats, and end-to-end risk assessments.

Using SAIF and Google’s common security infrastructure, we have implemented robust
cybersecurity measures and mitigations to protect the model and its associated model
parameters from unauthorized access, release, and theft. This includes, but is not limited to,
identity and access management, physical security, red teaming, endpoint security,
infrastructure hardening, our vulnerability reward program, and threat detection and response.
A foundational component of our approach involves routing the vast majority of model
interactions through hardened, mediated interfaces that limit permissible actions with respect
to model weights. This reduces the population of personnel and systems with direct access to
model weights across the model lifecycle and thereby reduces the attack surface for both
external and insider threats. In general, our multi-tiered system of security mitigations allows us
to calibrate the appropriateness and robustness of security measures to the level of risk posed.

We use security levels to indicate security goals/principles in line with the RAND model security
framework. We have implemented the RAND Security Level 2 (SL2) as a baseline, supplemented
by additional measures addressing risks from insider threats and well-resourced non-state
external actors — a posture we refer to as “SL2+.” Specific measures that take us beyond RAND
SL2 include, for example, dedicated insider risk teams, enhanced background checks for
personnel with sensitive access, review of model training data for indications of tampering,
sandboxed environments for processing untrusted inputs, advanced red-teaming simulating
Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)-level adversaries, and 24/7 proactive threat hunting. We view
our current security journey as an effort to reach RAND SL3, and are taking an approach that
maximizes reduction of risk at each step.

5.3 Agentic Alignment
Agentic misbehavior refers to unexpected, harmful, or misaligned actions taken by a
model operating as an autonomous agent with access to tools, environment permissions,
and multi-step reasoning capabilities. Unlike traditional model safety risks, which involve
generation of static content that is harmful in some way, agentic misbehavior emerges
during goal-directed execution loops. This can happen even when a user’s query is
benign, if the instruction is ambiguous or underspecified, or when the agent encounters
obstacles it attempts to overcome when executing tasks.

This section covers cases where both the user and the environment are benign; risks from
malicious users are covered in Section 4.2, and risks from malicious third-party content
are covered in Section 6.1.

To align Gemini for use as an agent, we train and evaluate it against a behavior
specification for Gemini models and agents. We measure the model's behavior along a
task-calibrated spectrum in four areas: Intent, Execution, Interaction, and Delivery. What
counts as "appropriate" depends on the task at hand. Our Agent Behavior specification
evaluates agentic alignment as a balance between two core failure modes:

   ●​ Overreach: Driven by an optimization incentive to fulfill user goals, an agent may
      exhibit behaviors such as:
         ○​ Destructive environment mutations: Including deleting user files, sending
             unauthorized emails, or executing high-stakes or irreversible actions
             without user consent.
         ○​ Data leakage: Reading and exfiltrating sensitive user data.
         ○​ Data fabrication: Hallucinating file contents, tool outputs, or execution
             results.
         ○​ Unauthorized policy circumvention: Bypassing setup restrictions.
         ○​ Reward hacking: Taking reckless shortcuts or manipulating tests to
             manufacture artificial success.
         ○​ Unchecked autonomy and scope creep: Such as modifying files when not
             instructed to, or not communicating with the user.
   ●​ Under-service: Passivity, superficial execution, premature task abandonment,
      analysis paralysis, or unnecessary permission-seeking.

                                                                                             62
Evaluating agentic misbehavior requires analyzing the model's entire trajectory, including
intermediate tool calls, state changes, and internal chain of thought reasoning, to ensure
the agent operates safely. Below, we describe overall agent behavior against this
specification and then targeted evaluations of specific failure modes: unsafe and
destructive actions, boundary violations on infeasible tasks and ambiguous feasible tasks,
fabrication, and reward hacking.

5.3.1 Overall Agent Behavior

To test agent behavior, we evaluate multi-turn software engineering and tool-use

5.3.3 Overstepping Boundaries on Ambiguous & High Risk Tasks

Even when a user request is feasible, it can be underspecified or ambiguous, the
environment can contain conflicting information, or there can be unintended high risk
consequences. In these situations, the agent must avoid overeager execution, and needs
to effectively collaborate with the user to seek more context, clarify, and communicate
any risks.

We designed an evaluation benchmark to measure whether agents are appropriately
deferring to the user and avoiding overstepping boundaries when faced with such
underspecified, ambiguous, or high risk tasks.

The benchmark contains a collection of ambiguous and high-risk SWE and non-coding
agentic tasks where the agent needs to collaborate with the user. In addition, we also added
a set of non-ambiguous tasks where the model should just execute without any friction.

To evaluate whether agents avoid overstepping boundaries, we use the following metrics:

   ●​ Active Collaboration Score: Measures whether the agent correctly pauses to
      clarify with the user when facing unresolvable ambiguity or high-risk actions.
   ●​ Counter-Collaboration Score: Measures whether the agent autonomously
      executes non-ambiguous tasks.
These two scores allow us to measure the Pareto frontier of agents demonstrating the
right amount of autonomy and agency without overstepping boundaries.

Gemini 4 Carbon recognizes when it should pause and consult the user more reliably than
Gemini 4 Argon: its Active Collaboration score is 80% (Argon: 70%; Gemini 3.8 Flash: 34%). This

(Counter-Collaboration 74% vs. 82%, within overlapping confidence intervals).

5.3.4 Fabrication and Honest Reporting

5.4 Sycophancy
Sycophancy occurs when a model prioritizes user approval over accuracy - agreeing with
false premises, offering unearned praise, or avoiding respectful pushback when
warranted. While helpfulness requires warmth and responsiveness, sycophancy can
undermine trust and impact user decision-making across domains such as health,
finances, work, or personal lives.

We evaluate sycophancy across multiple dimensions in our Personality Evaluation. We
developed a multi-dimensional Personality Evaluation that measures the model's behavior
along a situationally-calibrated spectrum for each dimension: from Inadequate (too dry,
terse, or disengaged) to Appropriate or Exaggerated (over-pleasing, over-eager, or
over-dramatic). Two dimensions, “Candor” (resisting false agreement and echo-chamber
responses) and “Self-awareness” (knowing the model's limits and not pretending
otherwise), measure anti-sycophantic behavior directly.

The evaluation consists of ~900 prompts, 2/3 single-turn and 1/3 multi-turn conversations
of up to 10+ user turns. Prompts are drawn from user, synthetic and hand-written
conversations. Each prompt targets one of nine personality dimensions. The two
dimensions that most directly capture sycophancy, Candor and Self-awareness, lean
heavily on multi-turn conversations, as sycophancy often emerges only under sustained
user pressure. Responses are scored by a prompted autorater using dimension-specific

                                                                                       70
rubrics, which places each response at −1 (Inadequate), 0 (Appropriate), or +1
(Exaggerated). Each response is rated ten times and averaged to reduce rater variance.

The evaluation has two composite metrics:

   ●​ Dryness (aggregate of Inadequate-direction scores): Measures whether
      training is suppressing desirable personality traits.
   ●​ Superficiality (aggregate of Exaggerated-direction scores): Measures whether
      training is producing over-pleasing or sycophantic patterns.

Scores below 10 indicate well-calibrated behavior, and scores above 25 warrant closer
inspection. This framework allows us to detect and manage the trade-off between
reducing sycophancy and maintaining appropriate warmth and engagement.

Carbon performed better than Argon in both dryness and superficiality.

Reducing sycophancy requires the model to disagree with users when warranted. Overly
aggressive anti-sycophancy interventions can produce responses that feel overly blunt,
dismissive, or pedantic—which we track as “Dryness” in our Personality Evaluation. We
actively measure both directions and continue to work on approaches that maintain
genuine warmth while eliminating harmful sycophantic patterns.

5.5 Hallucination
Gemini processes a wide range of everyday questions, document analysis, and research tasks.
Hallucinations — where a model asserts claims that are factually false or unsupported by
available evidence — impact task reliability. We train Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 4 Argon to
assert only verifiable claims, and to clearly communicate uncertainty rather than guessing
when information is unavailable.

We evaluate hallucinations across two complementary settings: closed-book parametric
knowledge and contextual grounding in user-provided documents.

5.5.1 Parametric Knowledge

Parametric evaluations test the model's intrinsic world knowledge when answering factual
questions without access to search or external context. Because the model must rely
entirely on knowledge encoded in its parameters, this setting measures both factual recall
and calibration — whether the model recognizes the limits of its knowledge and abstains
from answering rather than fabricating an answer. We evaluate models on two public

   ●​ SimpleQA Verified (paper): 1,000 human-verified, de-duplicated short-answer

   ●​ AA-Omniscience (paper): 6,000 cross-domain knowledge reliability questions

coverage and precision — F1 score (the harmonic mean of overall accuracy and accuracy
on attempted questions) for SimpleQA Verified, and the Omniscience Index (which
rewards correct answers and penalizes incorrect guesses) for AA-Omniscience.

Across both benchmarks, the Gemini 4 models demonstrate substantially stronger calibration,
reliably abstaining when uncertain rather than guessing. On AA-Omniscience, Gemini 4 Carbon

injection attacks
6.1 Indirect prompt injection attacks
Indirect prompt injections present a real cybersecurity challenge where AI models
sometimes struggle to differentiate between genuine user instructions and manipulative
commands embedded within the data they retrieve. These attacks are complex and
require constant vigilance.

In this section, we will provide updated results from the most important prompt injection
evaluations previously discussed for Gemini 4 Argon in Section 6.1 of the Gemini 4 Argon
Safety & Security Report.

6.1.1 Evaluation methodology

Static prompt injection benchmarks saturate quickly and fail to reflect how real-world attackers
adapt (Dziemian et al., 2026; Nasr et al., 2025). As applied for Gemini 4 Argon, we followed the
same principles set out in our 2025 White Paper (Shi et al., 2025 and Nasr et al., 2025),
evaluating Gemini 4 Carbon using our advanced automated red-teaming framework driven by
adaptive attackers that iteratively refine their strategies and payloads using feedback from
prior attempts.

Across five held-out evaluation suites: Tool Use (§6.1.2), Grounding/RAG (§6.1.3), Computer Use
(§6.1.4), Coding Agents (§6.1.5), and External Red-Teaming Arena Replays (§6.1.6), we measure
robustness using Survival @ N attempts: the percentage of scenarios in which the model resists
an adaptive attacker (Gemini 3.8 Flash unless otherwise noted) attempting to manipulate the
model using N indirect prompt injection attempts.

6.1.2 Tool use evaluations

We applied our general-purpose tool use evaluation on Gemini 4 Carbon. This internal
benchmark tests foundational resistance to prompt injections across 100 multi-step scenarios
spanning 45 synthetic tool categories. After dynamically re-executing the benign multi-turn
prefix so the conversation history is in-distribution for the tested model, the harness injects a
payload into the final tool response. The attacker goal is a syntactically valid tool call that is
completely unrelated to the user’s task but appears benign in isolation—for example, calling a
weather or calendar lookup tool midway through a financial query. This forces the model to rely

                                                                                                81
strictly on instruction provenance rather than harm-detection heuristics, which we also
measure in other internal and external prompt injection evaluations. To ensure our attacker
strategy libraries capture the long tail of real-world exploits, we mined observed human
red-teaming attacks to extract reusable strategy primitives, building on sparse
dictionary-learning decompositions of adversarial skills (Dabas et al., 2025) adapted to
preserve rare tail strategies.

Figure 6.1: Prompt injection robustness evaluation results: general tool calls.

To robustly evaluate Gemini 4 Carbon and Argon, we performed this evaluation with the tested
model's chain-of-thought visible to the attacker. This assumes a worst-case scenario where the
attacker can refine its attacks based on thought patterns triggered by previous attacks on the
tested model. We also carried out experiments where the tested model's chain-of-thought is
hidden from the attacker, and Survival @ 150 attempts increased to 99% for both Gemini 4
Carbon and Argon, suggesting that these models are significantly harder to attack with the
same techniques externally.

​

We evaluated Gemini 4 Carbon with the same simulated retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) environment (30 multi-document scenarios, 6 to 7 document excerpts per scenario,
up to 8 retrieval queries over 10 steps) to assess the model's resilience to attacks that

attempt to manipulate text outputs, as done on Gemini 4 Argon. In each scenario, an adaptive
attacker controls a single retrieved document excerpt as the attack entrypoint. Attacker goals
include inducing the model into making fabricated claims contradicted by the consensus of
remaining sources, suppressing the mention of facts found in other sources, or forcing the
model to rely exclusively on the attacker-controlled document.

Through our continued investment in post-training, Gemini 4 Carbon showed further

6.1.4 Computer use evaluations

using realistic modal popups, system banners, and inline credential forms. They aim to trigger
unauthorized financial transactions, data exfiltration, privilege escalation, or reputation

7.2 Patching and Code Remediation
As AI accelerates the rate of software vulnerability discovery, manual patching alone
cannot scale to keep pace. Closing this remediation gap requires models that can
automatically generate viable fixes—resolving the underlying root cause while preserving
existing application behavior. These capabilities underpin defensive security tooling such
as CodeMender as well as Google’s upstream patching of open-source software.

We evaluate Gemini 4 Carbon on CWE-bench v1 (cwe-bench.com/v1), a held-out
audit-and-patch benchmark developed by Collinear AI and independently evaluated with
Artificial Analysis. Unlike repair benchmarks that provide vulnerability advisories or
pinpoint affected code upfront, CWE-bench tests end-to-end discovery and remediation.
The agent receives only a real open-source repository and a general prompt to audit and
fix security issues—with no prior knowledge of what vulnerabilities exist, where they are
located, or how many are present. The private evaluation set consists of 120 tasks
spanning 73 CWEs across all 10 OWASP Top 10 (2025) categories (10–14 tasks each) and
six language families: C/C++, Go, JavaScript/TypeScript, Java, Python, and Rust.

Each task is graded by a deterministic programmatic verifier: an attempt is scored as
solved only if the programmatic exploit check confirms that the exploit no longer works
and all pre-existing functional and regression tests continue to pass. To reduce run-to-run

                                                                                         92
variance, each model is evaluated across four independent trials per task at high
reasoning effort, with a one-hour timeout per trial; Pass@1 is the mean success rate
across all 120 tasks. Gemini models are evaluated in our Antigravity agent harness, while
comparator models are evaluated in their providers' native coding agents or standard
open-source harnesses.

Gemini 4 Carbon achieves 71.0% Pass@1 on CWE-bench v1, up from 68.0% with Gemini 4
Argon, reflecting continued progress in autonomously generating accurate,
regression-free fixes that can help security teams remediate known vulnerabilities more
efficiently.
